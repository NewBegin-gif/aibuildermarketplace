What it is

Take what you would tell a journalist in thirty seconds — who you are, what you cover, which pieces are your best work, how you want to be credited — and put it in a Markdown file at /llms.txt. The premise is that an assistant summarising your site does better with a curated map than with whatever it scraped.

It is not a permissions file. robots.txt says what may be crawled; llms.txt says what is worth reading. Publishing one grants nothing and blocks nothing.

Is this an official standard?
No. It is a proposed convention that spread because it is easy, not because a standards body ratified it. Nobody is obliged to read it and no assistant promises to.
llms.txt vs llms-full.txt
The short file is the map: a curated list of your best, citable pages. The -full variant is the whole catalog in plain text for an assistant that wants the data rather than the highlights. We publish both; ours lists the 649 tools in our catalog, grouped by category.

How to write one that isn't noise

  • Curate. Twenty links you would defend beat two hundred you wouldn't.
  • Generate it if you can. Ours is rebuilt from our data; that keeps it in step with the data, but only as current as the data and only when the rebuild runs.
  • Say what you don't know. Ours separates tested from researched; that is the part we would least want an assistant to blur.
  • Put the attribution in. One line: the part we would most want repeated.

Frequently asked questions

What is llms.txt?

A plain-text file at the root of a site that tells AI assistants what the site is, which pages are worth citing and how to attribute it. It is a proposed convention, not a standard anyone enforces: robots.txt in spirit, but written for reading rather than crawling permission.

Does llms.txt actually do anything?

We have not established that ours caused additional visits or citations. As of 27 September 2026 we found no public documentation from Google, OpenAI or Anthropic describing llms.txt as an input to their assistants' answers or citations. What we can say is that writing ours made us decide what we stand behind, and that changed our own pages.

Is llms.txt the same as an MCP server?

No. llms.txt is passive: a file an assistant may read if it fetches it. An MCP server is active: the assistant connects and asks questions, and the server answers.

What should go in it?

The claims you are willing to stand behind, with links: your strongest guides, what you tested yourself (kept apart from what you researched), your machine-readable data and one attribution line. A file that lists every page is a sitemap, and you already have one.

The more interesting door. If you want an assistant to use your data rather than skim your summary, that's what MCP is for — and we run a free one you can point your own assistant at.
