What is the catch with Scarcity.AI?

All three read real data, which is worth saying before anything else — the low stock counter shows inventory levels and the visitors counter reads unique visitors, live traffic and product views. Nothing here invents a number. But the visitors counter carries two settings that shape which true number a shopper sees. It can show the counter only after a set number of views, so a quiet product simply displays nothing rather than a small figure. And the time frame is yours to set, so 'twelve people viewed this' can mean twelve in five minutes or twelve since yesterday, presented identically. Neither setting fabricates anything; together they let you present a true number that a reasonable shopper would read as something else. That distinction is where the legal line sits. The FTC treats false urgency and scarcity as deceptive practice under section 5 of the FTC Act, and the examples in its dark-patterns guidance are close to these mechanics: a low-stock claim when stock is plentiful, or a cart-activity figure well above the real one. The test is whether a reasonable consumer would be misled about a material fact. That test does not apply to the app; it applies to how you configure it, which means the compliance question lands on your desk rather than the developer's.

What is the catch with Seller Investigators?

Contingency pricing is the honest kind — you pay only out of money that arrived — but 25% of a recovery is not the same as 25% of a profit, and there is one question that decides whether this is worth it for you. A free audit that finds $20,000 sounds decisive until you learn how much of it was already on its way. Ask for the split, in writing, before the audit turns into an engagement.
