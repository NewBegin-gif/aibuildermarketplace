What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

AISQ (the Squirrly family) evolves its pricing and product names often — the honest advice is to buy only what's currently priced in front of you, in small credit batches first, and judge output on YOUR niche keywords before committing to bundles. Marketing automation that promises to cut workload 90% still needs your strategy and editing; credits generate drafts, not customers.

When the paid plan is worth it

AISQ's Squirrly SEO plugin uses a freemium split. The Free plan ($0/month) caps you at 1 site, 5 keyword researches per month, and 1 audit suite page per month, which is enough to evaluate the plugin but not to run a live blog. PRO ($9.99/month) is where an active site crosses over: 5 sites, 50 keyword researches per month, and 100 audit suite pages per month. The real upgrade trigger, though, is rank tracking. Both Free and PRO show 0 SERP checker credits, so you only get position tracking on Web Dev Kit ($51.20/month, 2000 credits, 10 sites) or Business ($71.99/month, 3000 credits, 7 sites) — Web Dev Kit costs less than Business and covers more sites, while Business carries more SERP credits (3000 vs 2000). Agencies managing many clients look instead at Meteor ($400/month flat). What paying does not fix is the interface: upgrading buys quota and credits, not the clearer workflow that a WordPress.org reviewer found missing.

How to actually use AISQ

First value runs through WordPress. Install Squirrly SEO from the plugin directory, activate it, then create and connect a free Squirrly Cloud account. That connection is mandatory, and it is what unlocks the audit, keyword research, and rank-tracking tools. You then pick recommended or expert mode and work through 5 configuration sections: social accounts, connecting Google Analytics and Search Console, webmaster verification, JSON-LD schema, and the XML sitemap. The friction concentrates in two places. First, the multiple Google authentication flows plus a wall of settings marked "optional" leave beginners unsure what is actually required. Second, one WordPress.org reviewer reports the guided flow breaking: the review says the plugin "stops dead when a new blog post opens" and refers to "menus that are either hidden or don't exist," with that user abandoning it after 3 hours for a competitor. In that same thread, the vendor's reply points the stuck user to its "14 Days Journey to Better Rankings" course to learn the plugin step-by-step.
