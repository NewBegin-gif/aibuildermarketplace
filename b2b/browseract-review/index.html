What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

The yearly toggle wears a -20% badge and the crossed-out prices suggest much more, but read what you would actually be charged on 13 September 2026: monthly is $16, $70 and $120, and yearly is $13, $56 and $96, so paying month to month costs roughly 20 to 25 percent more -- not the doubling the struck-through $20, $100 and $200 imply. The free tier is the bigger catch: its card now reads 200 signup credits where it read 1,500 on 2 September, which at 5 credits a workflow step is 40 steps, while the vendor's own llms.txt still advertises 2,000.

And the 'as low as' figures under the meters do not come from these tiers either: at Advanced a credit works out to about $0.00096, so a 5-credit workflow step is roughly $0.0048, not the advertised $0.0032 -- that lower number must belong to a credit pack whose price the page does not show. In the hands-on run of 4 September the meter was honest at account level (1,500 to 1,174 credits over one build and two runs, reconciling exactly with the history list), but the same Bot cost 21 credits on one run and 27 on the next for identical output, and the task panel briefly reported 0 credits for a run the list priced at 21.

What buyers report about BrowserAct

Recurring themes from the buyer-review sites listed at the foot of this page, read in September 2026. We use BrowserAct ourselves, but a single account is a narrow sample, so here is what other buyers report. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • Extraction in plain language is the thing reviewers name first: several describe pointing it at messy, unstructured pages that defeated their own scripts, with one news-site operator reporting more than half his prompt debugging time gone.
  • • Setup without code. Reviewers who had previously cobbled together Python scrapers or fought with Apify describe getting a working integration out of the interface alone, and say it has kept running unattended since.
  • • Session handling on logged-in sites: parallel sessions that do not cross-contaminate, sessions that survive past an hour, CAPTCHAs solved automatically, and output that arrives already structured.
What they complain about
  • • Documentation is the most consistent complaint. G2's own summary of the review set names it, and reviewers ask for more tutorials and guidance for newcomers.
  • • Setting up a scrape behind a login is described as cumbersome by one reviewer: enter credentials, confirm the login, wait for the refresh, with no native workflow for what is a common scenario.
  • • Price comes up, though the reviewer who raises it concludes the feature set justifies it rather than that it is too expensive.

Where the ratings disagree. The reviews disagree with each other about logged-in sites, and the split is worth reading before you buy on that promise. One reviewer calls unbroken login the single most powerful feature and reports sessions holding for over an hour; another calls setting up the same thing cumbersome and wants a native workflow for it. Both are September-2026-era reviews of the same product, so the likeliest reading is that it works well once configured and that the configuring is the rough part.

When the paid plan is worth it

On the monthly tab the list prices are struck through: Basic bills $16 against a crossed-out $20, Essential $70 against $100, Advanced $120 against $200. The yearly tab drops those to $13, $56 and $96 ($156, $672 and $1,152 up front), so the real monthly-versus-yearly spread is 20 to 25 percent, not the doubling the crossed-out prices suggest. Essential is where the plan stops being a toy: 50,000 credits is 10,000 workflow steps a month against Basic's 2,000, and 25 static proxies instead of 5.

At $56 yearly that is about $0.0011 a credit against $0.0013 on Basic, so the middle tier is also the cheaper credit; Advanced at $96 buys 100,000 credits and 40 simultaneous tasks at about $0.00096. What upgrading does not solve is the metering. Residential proxy traffic costs 5,000 credits a gigabyte at every tier, and one gigabyte-heavy job eats half of Essential's monthly allowance while the step count stays trivial, so size the plan to proxy traffic, not steps.

Static proxy pricing is not published at any tier, the page states no rate for credits that run out mid-task beyond saying packs exist, and no tier lifts the cap on what a credit buys.

What we found running BrowserAct ourselves

Hands-on · 2026-09-04

We ran the reproducible test BrowserAct's own partner team proposed, on a free account with the 1,500 sign-up credits. The prompt, verbatim: open books.toscrape.com, collect the first 20 books in listing order, open each detail page and return full title, displayed price, availability and source URL, leave unavailable fields blank, infer nothing. Before touching BrowserAct we fetched the same 20 pages with plain HTTP and saved the four fields as ground truth, so every value below was checked against the source, not against BrowserAct's own claims. Then we built the Bot from the prompt, ran it twice from the published version, and read the credit meter after each step.

  • Accuracy: 80 of 80 fields correct, three times over. The build's own verification output, run 1 and run 2 each returned 20 rows with title, price, availability and URL identical to our ground truth, including the parenthetical stock counts ('In stock (22 available)') and the currency symbol, which the Bot kept as displayed rather than converting.
  • The build costs more than the runs. Building the Bot took 4 minutes 59 seconds and 278 credits; the agent inspected the listing and a detail page, wrote a small package (a SKILL.md, a run.py and two DOM helpers), compiled it, and ran it end to end before declaring it ready. On the free tier that is about a fifth of the sign-up credits spent before the first real run.
  • Runs are cheap but not deterministic in cost. Run 1 used 21 credits in 1 minute 56 seconds; run 2, the same Bot on the same 20 pages, used 27 credits in 3 minutes 12 seconds. Identical output, 29% more credits and 65% more wall time. At Basic's 10,000 credits for $13 a month on yearly billing, a run of this size is roughly three cents, so the variance is small in money and worth knowing about in budgeting.
  • The meter is honest at the account level and confusing at the task level. Balance went 1,500 to 1,222 after the build, 1,201 after run 1 and 1,174 after run 2, which reconciles exactly with the per-task figures in the history list. But the task detail panel showed 'Credits used: 0' for a run that the history list, seconds later, priced at 21. Trust the list and the balance, not the panel.
  • The agent made one silent design decision. The prompt said 'the first 20 books', and the Bot was built with no parameters and 'first 20' hard-coded; it says so in its own summary and offers to change it on request. Fine for this test, but a Bot you intend to reuse should be built with the count as an input, and you have to ask for that.
  • The pricing you see inside the app is not the pricing on the website. On first login the app opened a '7-days free trial' panel listing Basic at $16, Essential at $70 and Advanced at $120 a month, with $20, $100 and $200 struck through, plus a trial of 1,000 or 1,500 credits on each. The public pricing page, re-read the day before, lists $20, $100 and $200 monthly. That is a 20 to 40 percent in-app discount that browseract.com/pricing does not mention.
  • What this test does not show. books.toscrape.com is a static sandbox with no login, no JavaScript rendering and no anti-bot measures, so none of the managed-browser, residential-proxy or CAPTCHA infrastructure in BrowserAct's pitch was exercised. It proves the no-code loop from plain prompt to reusable Bot; it says nothing about hostile sites, which is where the credit meters (5,000 credits per gigabyte of residential traffic) start to matter.

How to actually use BrowserAct

Spend the signup credits on measurement, not output, and know how few there are: the free card now reads 200 credits with 2 free Agent Builds and 2 simultaneous tasks, where in early September it read 1,500. BrowserAct's own llms.txt still says 2,000 -- a tenfold disagreement between two vendor documents, so check the number in your account first. At 200 credits the meters decide what you can even try: a workflow step is 5 credits, a local fingerprint browser 100, a gigabyte of residential proxy traffic 5,000.

That last figure decides your tier, and it is out of reach on the free tier. Start from the Agent Built route instead of the visual workflow builder -- you describe the site, records, fields and conditions in plain language and BrowserAct explores and tests the live website before it builds the bot. Decide the output early, whether CSV, JSON, the API, a webhook or Make, n8n and Zapier, because it shapes how the output fields are defined. The docs and a Discord sit behind the same signup if the build stalls.

Frequently asked questions

How many free credits do I actually get on signup?

Two of BrowserAct's own documents still disagree, a week after we first recorded it. On 20 September 2026 the pricing card reads 200 Free Credits on Signup plus 2 Free Agent Builds, 2 simultaneous tasks and 5 free local browsers. browseract.com/llms.txt, the file the vendor publishes for machines to read, still says the Free Trial carries 2,000 free credits — a tenfold difference on the same day. That same llms.txt names the pricing page as the source of truth for current amounts, so believe the card. Then do the arithmetic it implies: at 5 credits a workflow step, 200 credits is 40 steps, and a single local fingerprint browser costs 100 of them. On 2 September the card read 1,500.

Is paying monthly really twice the price of paying yearly?

No, and the struck-through numbers are doing the work of making it look that way. On 20 September 2026 the monthly tab charges $16, $70 and $120 with $20, $100 and $200 crossed out, while the yearly tab is $13, $56 and $96 a month ($156, $672 and $1,152 a year). Both tabs are unchanged from 13 September. So month-to-month costs roughly 20 to 25 percent more than annual — not the doubling the crossed-out list prices suggest. Those list prices are the anchor for a discount that has been running continuously since at least early September; nobody is currently offered $200 a month.

What does a run actually cost?

BrowserAct publishes the meters, which is more than most: a workflow step is 5 credits, a local fingerprint browser 100 credits, residential proxy traffic 5,000 credits per GB, static proxies are priced by location and tier with no figure shown, and the cloud browser is free for a limited time. Be careful with the 'as low as' rates underneath — $0.0032 a step, $0.064 a browser, $3.20 a GB. All three imply a credit costing $0.00064, but the cheapest plan on the page, Advanced billed yearly at $96 a month for 100,000 credits, works out at $0.00096. The advertised floor is about a third cheaper than any published tier can deliver, so it must belong to a credit pack whose price the page does not show. Read on browseract.com/pricing and browseract.com/llms.txt, 20 September 2026; the per-credit arithmetic is ours.

What should I weigh before buying Developer Tools software?

Read the documentation before you commit, because it is the most honest preview of the product. Look for a quickstart you can actually finish, error responses that are documented rather than guessed at, published rate limits and a clear statement of what happens when you hit them, and a changelog showing the API is still maintained. Vague limits usually mean you will discover them in production.
