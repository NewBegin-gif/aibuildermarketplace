Pricing (2026)

A credit-metered subscription with a free tier. Free Trial is $0 with 1,500 credits on signup and 5 free local browser profiles. Basic is $13 a month on yearly billing ($156 a year) or $20 month-to-month, for 10,000 credits a month, 10 simultaneous tasks, up to 5 static proxies and 10 local browsers. Essential is $56 a month yearly ($672) or $100 monthly, for 50,000 credits, 20 tasks, 25 static proxies and 50 local browsers.

Advanced is $96 a month yearly ($1,152) or $200 monthly, for 100,000 credits, 40 tasks, 50 static proxies and 100 local browsers. Every paid tier carries a 7-day free trial (1,000, 1,500 and 2,000 credits respectively) and access to extra credit packs. The meters are published on the same page: a workflow step costs 5 credits, a local fingerprint browser 100 credits, residential proxy traffic 5,000 credits per GB; static proxies are priced by location and tier without a figure, and the cloud browser is free for a limited time.

Seen inside the app on 2026-09-04, on first login: a trial panel offering Basic at $16, Essential at $70 and Advanced at $120 a month with the public monthly prices struck through, i.e. an in-app discount of 20 to 40 percent that the pricing page does not show. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Read the other way, paying month to month costs up to twice the yearly rate. And the 'as low as' figures under the meters do not come from these tiers either: at Advanced a credit works out to about $0.00096, so a 5-credit workflow step is roughly $0.0048, not the advertised $0.0032 -- that lower number must belong to a credit pack whose price the page does not show. In our own test the meter itself was honest at account level (1,500 to 1,174 credits over one build and two runs, reconciling exactly with the history list), but the same Bot cost 21 credits on one run and 27 on the next for identical output, and the task panel briefly reported 0 credits for a run the list priced at 21.

What BrowserAct does

No-code web scraper and browser-automation bots from one prompt, with managed browsers, residential proxies and CAPTCHA solving in one credit pool. Free tier with 1,500 credits; Basic $13/month on yearly billing ($20 monthly) for 10,000 credits, up to Advanced $96/$200 for 100,000. Monthly billing costs up to twice the yearly rate.

When the paid plan is worth it

Essential is where the plan stops being a toy: 50,000 credits is 10,000 workflow steps a month against Basic's 2,000, and 25 static proxies instead of 5. At $56 yearly that is about $0.0011 a credit versus $0.0013 on Basic, so the middle tier is also the cheaper credit. Advanced at $96 buys 100,000 credits and 40 simultaneous tasks; the per-credit price falls again to $0.00096. Nothing on the page says what happens when a task runs out of credits mid-way except that packs exist, so size the plan to the proxy traffic, not to the step count.

What we found running BrowserAct ourselves

Hands-on · 2026-09-04

We ran the reproducible test BrowserAct's own partner team proposed, on a free account with the 1,500 sign-up credits. The prompt, verbatim: open books.toscrape.com, collect the first 20 books in listing order, open each detail page and return full title, displayed price, availability and source URL, leave unavailable fields blank, infer nothing. Before touching BrowserAct we fetched the same 20 pages with plain HTTP and saved the four fields as ground truth, so every value below was checked against the source, not against BrowserAct's own claims. Then we built the Bot from the prompt, ran it twice from the published version, and read the credit meter after each step.

  • Accuracy: 80 of 80 fields correct, three times over. The build's own verification output, run 1 and run 2 each returned 20 rows with title, price, availability and URL identical to our ground truth, including the parenthetical stock counts ('In stock (22 available)') and the currency symbol, which the Bot kept as displayed rather than converting.
  • The build costs more than the runs. Building the Bot took 4 minutes 59 seconds and 278 credits; the agent inspected the listing and a detail page, wrote a small package (a SKILL.md, a run.py and two DOM helpers), compiled it, and ran it end to end before declaring it ready. On the free tier that is about a fifth of the sign-up credits spent before the first real run.
  • Runs are cheap but not deterministic in cost. Run 1 used 21 credits in 1 minute 56 seconds; run 2, the same Bot on the same 20 pages, used 27 credits in 3 minutes 12 seconds. Identical output, 29% more credits and 65% more wall time. At Basic's 10,000 credits for $13 a month on yearly billing, a run of this size is roughly three cents, so the variance is small in money and worth knowing about in budgeting.
  • The meter is honest at the account level and confusing at the task level. Balance went 1,500 to 1,222 after the build, 1,201 after run 1 and 1,174 after run 2, which reconciles exactly with the per-task figures in the history list. But the task detail panel showed 'Credits used: 0' for a run that the history list, seconds later, priced at 21. Trust the list and the balance, not the panel.
  • The agent made one silent design decision. The prompt said 'the first 20 books', and the Bot was built with no parameters and 'first 20' hard-coded; it says so in its own summary and offers to change it on request. Fine for this test, but a Bot you intend to reuse should be built with the count as an input, and you have to ask for that.
  • The pricing you see inside the app is not the pricing on the website. On first login the app opened a '7-days free trial' panel listing Basic at $16, Essential at $70 and Advanced at $120 a month, with $20, $100 and $200 struck through, plus a trial of 1,000 or 1,500 credits on each. The public pricing page, re-read the day before, lists $20, $100 and $200 monthly. That is a 20 to 40 percent in-app discount that browseract.com/pricing does not mention.
  • What this test does not show. books.toscrape.com is a static sandbox with no login, no JavaScript rendering and no anti-bot measures, so none of the managed-browser, residential-proxy or CAPTCHA infrastructure in BrowserAct's pitch was exercised. It proves the no-code loop from plain prompt to reusable Bot; it says nothing about hostile sites, which is where the credit meters (5,000 credits per gigabyte of residential traffic) start to matter.

How to actually use BrowserAct

Spend the signup credits on measurement rather than output. The free tier arrives with 1,500 credits and the meters are published, so before you pick a plan, build one bot against the site you actually care about and read what a single run costs: a workflow step is 5 credits, a local fingerprint browser 100, and a gigabyte of residential proxy traffic 5,000. That last figure is the one that decides your tier.

Start from the Agent Built route instead of the visual workflow builder — you describe the site, records, fields and conditions in plain language and BrowserAct explores and tests the live website before it builds the bot. The template gallery already has bots for YouTube comments, Amazon best sellers, Google Maps business listings and Reddit posts. Decide the output early, whether CSV, JSON, the API, a webhook or Make, n8n and Zapier, because it shapes how the bot's output fields are defined. The docs and a Discord sit behind the same signup if the build stalls.

What should I weigh before buying Developer Tools software?

Read the documentation before you commit, because it is the most honest preview of the product. Look for a quickstart you can actually finish, error responses that are documented rather than guessed at, published rate limits and a clear statement of what happens when you hit them, and a changelog showing the API is still maintained. Vague limits usually mean you will discover them in production.
