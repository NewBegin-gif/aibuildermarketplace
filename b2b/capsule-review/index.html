What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

The honest flip side — advanced automation, deep reporting and integrations thin out at the top tiers, and a scaling sales org can outgrow it. Buy it to finally have a CRM the team fills in; plan a migration path if you're heading toward enterprise process complexity.

What Capsule does

Deliberately simple CRM for small businesses: contacts, pipeline and tasks. Free for 250 contacts and two users, then about $21/user/month on Starter. The simplicity is the product — advanced automation, deep reporting and integrations thin out, and a scaling sales org can outgrow it.

When the paid plan is worth it

The free plan is a working two-person CRM rather than a demo: 250 contacts, 1 sales pipeline and 5 custom fields. It stops being viable once the team passes the 2-user maximum or the list outgrows 250 records; the cheapest paid tier is Starter, US$21 per user per month billed monthly, US$18 billed annually. The more consequential line is Starter to Growth (US$38 monthly, US$36 annual). Workflow automations, multiple sales pipelines, project boards, team and access controls, reporting dashboards and the AI enrichment and summary tools all sit at Growth and above.

Starter itself adds 30,000 contacts, email templates, a shared mailbox, basic reporting, premium integrations, goals, custom activity types and the AI Pipeline Generator. Pin those premium integrations down before you pick a tier, because Capsule marks them one by one: its Xero, Sage, FreshBooks and Kashflow pages all read Starter and above, while FreeAgent and Zapier are listed on every plan including Free. A free account can still reach its accounts package, but through FreeAgent or a Zap rather than the direct Xero link.

Advanced (US$60 monthly, US$54 annual) mostly raises ceilings, 120,000 contacts, 50 sales pipelines and 50 project boards, so it repays only organisations genuinely running parallel processes. Ultimate sits above Advanced for teams of five or more and adds 240,000 contacts, a dedicated account manager, custom training and ongoing priority support. Its price is the one number you cannot take off the page: we recorded US$75 monthly and US$72 annual reading it on 31 July, but on 11 September the card printed no amount at all and sent us to a trial and a sales conversation instead, so treat the top of the ladder as quoted rather than published.

What no tier changes: every seat costs the same regardless of how lightly it is used, and marketing automation sits outside the product as a separate paid add-on.

What we found running Capsule ourselves

Hands-on · 2026-09-07

We ran the import ourselves instead of summarising Capsule's help pages. Daan created the account; everything after that was measured in the product. We built a deliberately messy CSV — 24 rows, one row repeated with a different job title and phone number, and one row carrying nothing but an organisation name — imported it with “Import and update duplicates” and the overwrite box ticked, and then tried to undo it. The account was a fresh one on the 14-day premium trial, so nothing below is a free-plan restriction.

  • 24 rows became 34 contacts. Capsule creates a separate Organisation record for every distinct organisation name in the file — 22 people plus 12 organisations. Nobody warns you, and it matters: the free plan stops at 250 contacts, so a 24-row spreadsheet costs 34 of them.
  • A row with only the Organisation column filled does build a company record on its own. Our “Solitary Holdings BV” row arrived as an organisation with its phone number attached.
  • The duplicate row wins. Anna Bakker went in twice, second time as “Head of Marketing” instead of “Marketing Lead”, and the record ended up with the later title. So the last occurrence in the file overwrites the earlier one, inside a single import.
  • But “overwrite” means exactly the four fields it names — name, job title, organisation and custom fields — and nothing else. The two rows carried different phone numbers and the contact now holds BOTH, sitting side by side. Contact channels are appended, not replaced, so a bad update pass leaves duplicate numbers on the record rather than wrong ones.
  • There is no undo, and that is worth knowing before you press Import. The import history logs “34 total imported, 34 contacts created” and offers exactly two things: View and Close. View opens a saved filter holding precisely those 34 contacts — which looks like the undo route until you select all of them and find the only bulk actions are Tag, Create Campaign and Export. No delete. Not on that list, not on the full contact list, not on the contact record, and not on its edit screen. We could not reach a delete anywhere in the interface.
  • So the practical rule is the opposite of reassuring: an import is one-way. Test on a handful of rows first, because the clean-up you are counting on does not exist where you would look for it.

How to actually use Capsule

Capsule starts empty, and the import screen is where the shaping happens. Import accepts CSV or Excel; a row carrying a first or last name becomes a Person, while a row with only the Organisation column filled builds the company record. Count on more records than rows: every distinct organisation name in the file also becomes its own Organisation, so our 24-row test file arrived as 34 contacts — worth knowing when the free plan stops at 250.

Duplicate rows can be imported as new, ignored, or used to update existing contacts, and the update option carries a second checkbox that spells out its own limits: it overwrites name, job title, organisation and custom fields. Nothing else. We imported the same person twice with different details and the later row's job title won, while both phone numbers ended up on the record side by side — contact channels are added, not replaced.

Treat the import as one-way. Capsule logs what it did and gives you a saved filter of exactly the contacts it created, which looks like an undo until you use it: the only bulk actions on that list are Tag, Create Campaign and Export, and we could not find a delete anywhere in the interface — not in bulk, not on the record, not on its edit screen. Test with a handful of rows before you commit the real file.

Notes are one-way too: they import onto new contacts only, and Excel truncates fields at 32,000 characters, ruling it out for long note histories.

Frequently asked questions

What does Capsule cost?

Free for 250 contacts and 2 users, then roughly $21, $38 and $60 per user a month for Starter, Growth and Advanced, read over a US connection. Ultimate, the fifth card, prints no price at all: it asks for a trial and a call, so the $75 we recorded on 31 July is history rather than a quote. The advertised annual saving moves too — up to 14% on the page in August, up to 26% when we looked on 11 September. The trial is 14 days with no card.

Is the free tier a real CRM?

For a solo consultant with a small book, yes: 250 contacts and two users covers a genuine pipeline rather than a demo. The ceiling is contacts, and 250 arrives faster than most people expect once you import an inbox.

Can I avoid the AI features?

Yes, and Capsule says so explicitly on the pricing page: the AI features can be switched off in your account. That is worth knowing for anyone with a client confidentiality concern or a policy against sending CRM content to a model.

What should I weigh before buying Growth & Revenue software?

It depends on whether you have the bottleneck it solves. Small teams get the most out of this category when one clear problem is already costing real hours or revenue; buying ahead of that just adds cost and another login. Price it against the hours or lost deals it removes, not against its feature list, and start on the smallest plan that covers the job.
