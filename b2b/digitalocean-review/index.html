DigitalOcean Review (2026)The $4 Server Is Just the Start

The $4 Droplet is real, but it is the smallest machine — 512 MiB of memory — and it is only the server.

Best for

developers and small teams who want to run an app, API or database on plain cloud infrastructure with published per-second prices, from a free static site on App Platform or a $4 Droplet up to managed Kubernetes and on-demand GPUs.

Skip it if

you want someone else to look after the server and the software on it — a managed host such as Cloudways, which DigitalOcean owns and lists from $11 a month on the same pricing page, does that for more money — or you do not want to administer a server at all.

DigitalOcean is pay-as-you-go cloud infrastructure: you rent the pieces and pay per piece. Basic Droplets (virtual servers) run from $4 a month for 512 MiB, 1 vCPU, 10 GiB of SSD and 500 GiB of outbound transfer, through $6 (1 GiB), $12 (2 GiB), $18, $24 and $48 up to $96 a month for 16 GiB and 8 vCPUs. Since 1 January 2026 Droplets are billed per second, with a minimum of 60 seconds or $0.01.

Backups are extra: 20% of the Droplet price for weekly or 30% for daily, and outbound transfer beyond the allowance is $0.01 per GiB. App Platform has a free tier for three static-site apps (1 GiB of transfer each, further static apps $3 each); apps with a server start at $5 a month for a fixed container with 1 vCPU and 512 MiB, limited to one instance, then $10, $12, $25 and $50, with a $7 development database, a $25 dedicated egress IP and $0.02 per GiB beyond the bandwidth allowance.

Full pricing notes (2 more paragraphs)

Managed databases start at $15 a month, managed Kubernetes at $12, load balancers at $12 and Spaces object storage at $5. GPU Droplets run on demand from $0.76 per GPU-hour (RTX 4000 Ada) through $1.57 (L40S) to $4.41 (H100) and $4.47 (H200); a 12-month reservation brings the H100 to $3.26, and the GPU page says new pricing took effect on 1 August 2026.

DigitalOcean also owns Cloudways and lists its managed hosting from $11 a month on the same pricing page. Billing is monthly in arrears: the card is charged on the first day of the month for the previous month's usage, or earlier if usage passes a threshold.

Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Backups cost 20% (weekly) or 30% (daily) of the Droplet price on top, a managed database starts at $15 a month and a load balancer at $12, so a small production app is several line items rather than one: a 2 GiB Droplet at $12 with daily backups ($3.60) and the smallest managed database ($15) comes to $30.60 a month before any bandwidth overage. Billing is per second and in arrears, which is fair, but the bill arrives after the month you used — set a billing alert on day one.

What DigitalOcean does

Developer cloud for running apps, APIs and databases: virtual servers (Droplets), a managed app platform, managed databases, Kubernetes, object storage and on-demand GPUs, billed per second.

Droplet or App Platform: who looks after the server

A $4 Droplet and a $5 App Platform container look almost the same on paper — 1 vCPU and 512 MiB each — but you are buying different jobs. The Droplet is a virtual machine: you install, secure and patch it yourself, and it comes with 10 GiB of SSD and 500 GiB of outbound transfer. The App Platform container is a managed runtime: you deploy from GitHub or GitLab, HTTPS and the CDN are included, and the paid tier adds automatic OS patching, with 50 GiB of transfer on the $5 plan and a limit of one instance on a fixed-CPU container.

Pay the extra dollar when you would rather not run a server; take the Droplet when you want root access and ten times the transfer allowance.

How to actually use DigitalOcean

Set a billing alert before you create anything. DigitalOcean bills in arrears on the first day of the month, or earlier if usage passes a threshold, and its own FAQ says you can be emailed when the bill crosses an amount you choose. Then match the product to the job: a static front end fits App Platform's free tier (three static-site apps, 1 GiB of transfer each); an app with a server starts on a $5 App Platform container or a Droplet from $4.

Switch on backups when you create a Droplet (30% of its price for daily), and add a managed database from $15 when you would rather not run your own.

Frequently asked questions

Is DigitalOcean free?

Partly. App Platform's free tier runs three static-site apps with 1 GiB of transfer each, Functions include 90,000 GiB-seconds a month at no charge, and the platform includes free DNS, cloud firewalls, a container registry with one repository and 500 MiB, and one uptime check a month. Anything with a server — a Droplet, a container app, a database — is paid, from $4 a month for the smallest Droplet. Read on digitalocean.com/pricing on 3 October 2026.

What does a small production app cost on DigitalOcean?

Count the line items, not the Droplet. On the prices we read on 3 October 2026: a 2 GiB Droplet is $12 a month, daily backups add 30% of that ($3.60), and the smallest managed database is $15, so $30.60 a month before any bandwidth overage at $0.01 per GiB. On App Platform the same idea starts from a $5 container plus a $7 development database.

How does DigitalOcean bill?

Monthly in arrears: on the first day of the month it charges the card for the previous month's usage, or earlier if usage passes a threshold. Droplets are billed per second since 1 January 2026, with a minimum of 60 seconds or $0.01. Adding a card can trigger a temporary pre-authorisation, typically $1, which DigitalOcean cancels.

What do GPUs cost on DigitalOcean?

On demand from $0.76 per GPU-hour for an RTX 4000 Ada up to $4.41 for an H100 and $4.47 for an H200 (read 3 October 2026; DigitalOcean says new GPU pricing took effect on 1 August 2026). A 12-month reservation brings the H100 to $3.26. An H100 left running on demand for a 720-hour month is $3,175.20.

How do I judge a developer tool's docs and rate limits?

Read the documentation before you commit, because it is the most honest preview of the product. Look for a quickstart you can actually finish, error responses that are documented rather than guessed at, published rate limits and a clear statement of what happens when you hit them, and a changelog showing the API is still maintained. Vague limits usually mean you will discover them in production.
