What Drip does

Ecommerce email and onsite marketing built around your store's order data — segments, abandoned-cart and welcome playbooks, pop-ups and A/B testing, with one-click links to Shopify, WooCommerce and BigCommerce. Priced purely on list size from $39/month for 2,500 people, 14-day trial without a card. Two things the headline price hides: chat support only starts at $99/month, and the promised unlimited sends turn into a hard cap of twelve emails per contact per month somewhere above 25,000 contacts (read 12 Aug 2026).

Pricing (2026)

✓ Pricing re-verified 12 Aug 2026

(2026-08-12, read off their own pricing calculator) One ladder, no tiers: you drag a slider to your list size and Drip prints a monthly price. 2,500 people is $39, then $49 at 3,000, $59 at 3,500, $69 at 4,000, $79 at 4,500, $89 at 5,000, $99 at 6,000, $109 at 7,000, $169 at 12,500, $369 at 25,000, $699 at 50,000, $1,299 at 110,000 and $1,949 at 175,000.

Above roughly 175,000 the number disappears and the page says “Let’s chat”, so the top of the range is quote-only. The trial is 14 days and takes no card, but Drip states unlimited sends are switched off during it. Two things the ladder does not advertise. Chat support is not included at the entry price: up to 5,000 people the line reads “Email support”, and “Chat and email support” only appears from $99 (6,000 people) — their FAQ says the same in words.

And “Unlimited email sends” stops being literally true as you climb: it still says unlimited at 25,000 people, but at 50,000 the same line reads “600,000 email sends”, at 110,000 “1,320,000” and at 175,000 “2,100,000” — in every case exactly twelve sends per contact per month. Every plan carries the same feature set otherwise, including up to 50 workflows and unlimited sub-accounts, so what you buy going up the ladder is list capacity, not capability. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

The honest knock

The word “unlimited” quietly expires. At the prices most small shops pay the send allowance really is uncapped, but from somewhere between 25,000 and 50,000 contacts the same bullet turns into a hard number — 600,000 sends at 50,000 people, twelve per contact per month — and Drip never flags the switch. If you run a daily-deal or high-frequency list, do the arithmetic before you migrate: twelve sends per contact is generous for a monthly newsletter and tight for anything that mails several times a week. The second thing to price in is support. The page sells one product at every level, but you cannot talk to anyone in chat until $99/month; below that it is email only, which is a real difference on the day a broken automation is sending to the wrong segment.

When the paid plan is worth it

There are no tiers to weigh up here, only a slider, so the useful question is a different one: where on that ladder do you get the least for your money? Work out the price per contact and the answer is uncomfortable for exactly the list size most small senders have. At 2,500 people you pay $39, which is 1.56 cents a contact. It gets steadily worse from there: 1.63 cents at 3,000, 1.73 at 4,000, and a peak of 1.78 cents at 5,000 people for $89. Then it improves again -- 1.65 cents at 6,000, 1.56 at 7,000, 1.35 at 12,500, and 1.11 cents at 175,000. A list of 5,000 is the single worst place to sit on this ladder, and growing past it makes each contact cheaper rather than dearer. Two things the slider does not advertise, and both bite at the bottom. Chat support is not included at the entry price: up to 5,000 people the line reads email support only, and chat appears from $99. And unlimited sends stops being literally true as you climb -- at 50,000 contacts the same line reads 600,000 sends, at 110,000 it reads 1,320,000, at 175,000 it reads 2,100,000. In every case that is exactly twelve sends per contact per month, which is a generous cap but a cap nonetheless. Everything else is identical at every price, including up to 50 workflows and unlimited sub-accounts. You are buying list capacity, not capability.

How to actually use Drip

Connect the store first, and know what that click does to the bill. Drip's own instructions are short -- Settings, Integrations, Shopify, install the app, pick the account to link -- but the sync brings across your whole customer list and order history, and the same article states plainly that your Drip billing increases based on the number of customers synced. On a price that is a slider tied to list size, that is your first invoice, decided in one step. Next comes the app embed, a JavaScript snippet you switch on from the integration page so Drip can track page visits and product views. Only then build a workflow. Triggers are forward-facing: Drip's guide says they apply only to people who meet the condition after the workflow is turned on, so nothing fires retroactively for the list you just imported. Use the 14-day trial for shape rather than sending -- unlimited emails are switched off during it. Distilled from Drip's help centre articles Connect Your Shopify Store (help.drip.com/hc/en-us/articles/4424695491725-Connect-Your-Shopify-Store) and Getting Started with Workflows (help.drip.com/hc/en-us/articles/4424702569357-Getting-Started-with-Workflows), plus the FAQ on drip.com/pricing, all read 19 August 2026.

Best for: an online store on Shopify, WooCommerce or BigCommerce that already has order data worth segmenting and wants ecommerce playbooks — abandoned cart, welcome series, post-purchase — without assembling them by hand. The pricing model suits a list that grows steadily and mails at a normal cadence, because you pay for people rather than per seat or per feature, and every plan carries the whole feature set.
Who should skip it: if you are not selling online: Drip says an ecommerce store is not required but that the platform is built for one, and you would be paying for the half you cannot use. Skip it too if your list is large and you mail often, because the send allowance caps at twelve per contact per month once you are past the small tiers, and if your list is heading beyond 175,000 people, where the published ladder ends and pricing becomes a conversation.

The natural comparison is Klaviyo — the comparison Drip picks itself — it publishes a Drip vs. Klaviyo page — and the one most ecommerce teams actually make. Both price on list size and both live off store data; the practical question is what the send allowance and the support line look like at your volume, which is where Drip's ladder is unusually easy to read and unusually quiet about the twelve-sends ceiling. Decide by which one fits the job above, not by the louder brand.
