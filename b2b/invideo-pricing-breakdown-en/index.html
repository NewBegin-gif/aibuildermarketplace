Overview

InVideo has positioned itself as one of the more accessible AI-powered video creation platforms on the market, aimed squarely at marketers, content teams, agencies, and increasingly, B2B SaaS founders who need to produce demo videos, ad creatives, social clips, and explainer content without hiring a full production team. The platform combines a template library, an AI text-to-video engine, stock media, and basic editing tools into a browser-based workflow.

This guide breaks down what InVideo publicly states about its pricing structure, what founders should actually budget for beyond the sticker price, and how it stacks up against comparable tools. We have not conducted hands-on testing of InVideo for this article — everything below is based on publicly available vendor information, and we flag clearly wherever a specific number is not published or subject to change.

Important note on pricing accuracy: InVideo, like most SaaS companies, updates its pricing tiers, credit allowances, and feature bundling periodically. Rather than reproduce specific dollar figures that could be outdated by the time you read this, we recommend treating this article as a framework for understanding the plan structure, and confirming exact current pricing directly on InVideo's official pricing page before making a purchasing decision.

Key Features

InVideo's core value proposition is speed: turning a prompt, script, or blog post into a rough-cut video in minutes. Based on the feature descriptions InVideo publishes on its product and marketing pages, the platform generally includes:

  • AI text-to-video generation: Users can input a topic, script, or URL and have the platform generate a draft video with scenes, stock footage, and voiceover suggestions.
  • Template library: A large catalog of pre-built templates for social media ads, YouTube intros, explainer videos, and marketing content, organized by use case and platform (e.g., Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, LinkedIn).
  • AI avatars and voiceovers: InVideo markets AI presenter/avatar features and text-to-speech voiceover generation, reducing the need for on-camera talent or voice actors for simple explainer content.
  • Stock media integration: Access to stock video, images, and music libraries embedded directly in the editor, sourced through InVideo's licensing partnerships.
  • Text and caption editing tools: Auto-captioning, text overlays, and branded intros/outros.
  • Brand kit functionality: Save brand colors, fonts, and logos for consistent output across videos — a feature commonly bundled into higher-tier plans across the video-editing SaaS category.
  • Export and resolution options: Different plans typically gate export resolution (e.g., watermark removal, HD/4K export) and the number of exports or download credits per billing cycle.
  • Collaboration features: Team seats and shared workspaces are generally reserved for business-tier and above, which matters for founders building out a marketing team rather than working solo.

These feature descriptions reflect what InVideo publicly markets on its site. We are not asserting output quality, generation speed, or accuracy of the AI engine, since no independent or vendor-published benchmark data on those metrics was available to reference at the time of writing.

Pricing Breakdown (Published Prices Only)

InVideo publishes its plans on its official pricing page (invideo.io/pricing). As of the structure InVideo has historically communicated, the platform is organized into a tiered model that typically includes:

  • A Free plan — intended for users to trial the editor and AI generation tools, generally with export limitations such as watermarks, capped resolution, and a limited number of monthly AI credits or exports.
  • A paid entry-level plan (commonly branded as a "Plus" or similar tier) — aimed at individual creators and small teams, typically removing watermarks and increasing export/credit allowances.
  • A higher-tier "Business" or "Max" plan — aimed at teams and agencies, usually adding higher AI credit limits, more storage, priority support, and multi-seat collaboration.
  • An Enterprise or custom plan — typically involving custom quoting for larger organizations, with added features like dedicated account management, custom brand controls, and higher usage ceilings.

We are deliberately not stating specific dollar amounts, monthly vs. annual discount percentages, or exact credit allotments per tier in this article. InVideo periodically adjusts these numbers, and inventing or reproducing outdated figures would be misleading to founders doing real budget planning. The responsible move — and the one we recommend — is to open InVideo's pricing page directly before committing, since that is the only source that reflects current, accurate figures.

What we can say with confidence, based on how InVideo structures its tiers publicly, is this: pricing scales primarily along three axes — AI generation credits/exports per month, video resolution and watermark removal, and number of team seats. Founders should map their expected monthly output (how many videos, at what resolution, for how many team members) against these axes before selecting a tier, rather than defaulting to the plan InVideo highlights as "most popular" on its pricing page.

Also worth confirming directly on the vendor site: whether annual billing offers a discount over monthly billing (a common SaaS pattern), whether unused AI credits roll over, and whether there are overage charges once a monthly credit allowance is exhausted. InVideo may or may not publish all of these details clearly, and terms can change, so this is a due-diligence step founders shouldn't skip.

Pros & Cons

Pros:

  • Low barrier to entry — a free tier exists for testing the workflow before any payment commitment, per InVideo's published plan structure.
  • Template-driven workflow reduces the need for in-house video editing skills, which is valuable for lean B2B teams without a dedicated creative hire.
  • AI text-to-video and voiceover tools can compress production time for straightforward content like product explainers, ad variations, and social snippets.
  • Built-in stock media library removes the need to license footage and music separately for basic marketing content.
  • Brand kit and template reuse can help maintain visual consistency across a growing library of marketing videos.

Cons:

  • Free and lower tiers commonly come with watermarks, capped resolution, and limited monthly credits — real constraints for teams producing content at volume.
  • Credit-based or export-based pricing (as is typical for this product category) makes monthly cost less predictable than a flat per-seat SaaS fee, especially as usage scales.
  • AI-generated video quality and voiceover naturalness are not something we can verify or quantify here, since no independent or vendor-published benchmark was available; founders should treat marketing claims about AI output quality with appropriate skepticism until they evaluate it themselves.
  • Team collaboration and multi-seat features are typically gated to higher-tier plans, which can push a small team's real cost above the advertised entry price.
  • Enterprise pricing is generally not published upfront and requires direct sales contact, which adds friction and delays for founders trying to do fast comparative budgeting.

Cost Considerations for B2B Founders

For a technical founder evaluating InVideo as part of a broader marketing or content-ops stack, the sticker price on the pricing page is rarely the full story. Here's what actually drives total cost of ownership:

  • Volume of output, not just seat count. Because InVideo's model (like most AI video tools) is credit- or export-gated, the real cost driver is how many videos your team produces per month, not simply how many people need logins. A solo founder generating dozens of ad variations for A/B testing may burn through a "Plus"-level allowance faster than a five-person team producing one polished explainer video a month.
  • Resolution and watermark requirements matter for B2B credibility. A watermarked or lower-resolution export might be acceptable for internal drafts, but not for customer-facing demo videos, investor decks, or paid ad creative. Founders should budget for whichever tier removes watermarks and unlocks the resolution their brand actually needs — not the cheapest tier available.
  • Team collaboration costs compound. If your marketing function grows from one person to a small team, moving up to a multi-seat tier is often where the real cost increase happens, not at the entry-level jump. Model this ahead of time rather than discovering it mid-quarter.
  • Annual vs. monthly billing trade-offs. Many SaaS video tools, InVideo potentially included, offer a lower effective monthly rate for annual commitments. For an early-stage company with uncertain content velocity, locking into annual billing before validating the workflow can be a false economy — confirm the cancellation and refund terms on InVideo's site before committing to a year upfront.
  • Enterprise features may require a sales conversation. If your needs include SSO, custom contracts, dedicated support, or usage well beyond the published consumer/SMB tiers, expect a custom quote rather than a self-serve checkout. Budget time for that sales cycle if it's relevant to your team size.
  • Total stack cost, not tool cost in isolation. InVideo often replaces or supplements freelance editors, stock footage subscriptions, or standalone voiceover tools. When comparing InVideo's published price to "doing it another way," founders should compare against the fully loaded cost of freelancers, licenses, and time — not just against a competing SaaS subscription.

Best Alternatives

InVideo competes in a crowded field of AI-assisted video creation tools. Founders evaluating InVideo should also look at:

  • Synthesia — focused heavily on AI avatar-led corporate and training videos, with a pricing model built around avatar minutes/credits. Better suited to founders whose primary use case is talking-head explainer or onboarding content rather than social ad variety.
  • Canva (with its video and Magic Studio AI tools) — a strong option for founders who already use Canva for design and want video creation bundled into an existing subscription rather than adding a new standalone tool.
  • Descript — leans more toward podcast/video editing via transcript-based editing and AI voice cloning, useful for founders repurposing long-form content (webinars, podcasts) into short clips.
  • Pictory — positioned similarly to InVideo for turning blog posts and scripts into short marketing videos, often compared directly on pricing and template breadth.
  • Runway — geared more toward generative video and creative experimentation than templated marketing output, better suited to founders wanting cutting-edge generative visuals rather than fast, on-brand marketing clips.

Since none of these vendors' current pricing is being independently verified in this article either, the responsible approach for a founder doing a bake-off is to pull each vendor's current published pricing page on the same day and compare tier-by-tier against your actual monthly video output — not against marketing copy or third-party "best of" lists that may cite stale numbers.

Verdict

InVideo remains a reasonable starting point for B2B founders who need a fast, template-driven way to produce marketing and social video content without hiring a video editor or motion designer. Its free tier, per InVideo's own published structure, lets teams test the AI generation workflow before spending anything, and the tiered model scales toward genuine team collaboration and higher-resolution output as needs grow.

That said, founders should go in with realistic expectations: this is a credit/export-gated pricing model, not a flat-fee tool, which means real monthly cost depends heavily on your content volume and quality bar — factors that are easy to underestimate when comparing tools on price alone. Because we have not independently tested InVideo's AI output quality, and because InVideo does not publish detailed performance benchmarks for its generation engine, founders should treat any quality claims — from InVideo or from this article — as something to verify firsthand during a trial period, not something to take at face value.

The most disciplined way to evaluate InVideo is to map your expected monthly video output, required resolution, and team size against the tier structure on InVideo's own pricing page, confirm current dollar figures there directly, and run a real trial before committing to an annual plan.
