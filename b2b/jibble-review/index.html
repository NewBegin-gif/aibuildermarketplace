Pricing (2026)

✓ Pricing re-verified 25 Sep 2026

Jibble still publishes no prices on its public site — jibble.io/pricing, /plans and /upgrade-plans land on the homepage — but the account carries a full price table behind its upgrade button. Four plans, per user per month: Free, €0, 'forever', for unlimited members; Essentieel €1.79 billed annually or €2.99 monthly; Groei €3.79 annually or €4.99 monthly; Pro €6.79 annually or €9.29 monthly (plan names as our Dutch-language account shows them).

Monthly billing costs 32% to 67% more than annual. A new organisation starts on Free with a 14-day trial of the paid features. We saw euros; a visitor elsewhere may be shown another currency. Earlier history: on 30 August 2026 the public pricing page had been retired, and on 2 September 2026 Jibble's partnerships team confirmed to us in writing that there is no external pricing page.

Jibble states an average of 4.8 out of 5 across major review platforms and app stores on more than 20,000 verified reviews. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

What the free plan really covers

Jibble's homepage claims a free forever plan with unlimited users, '100% free' and no credit card. In a category where per-seat billing is the norm that is unusual, and it makes the first question different from every other tool here: not which tier, but whether you need a tier at all. On 25 September 2026 we opened an account and found the prices the public site no longer shows. The upgrade button inside the app opens four plans, per user per month and excluding VAT: Free €0, Essentieel €1.79 on annual billing (€2.99 monthly), Groei €3.79 (€4.99), Pro €6.79 (€9.29).

Jibble's partnerships team had told us on 2 September that there is no external pricing page, and that is still true; the table simply lives behind the login. First, what the free tier excludes, now from Jibble's own comparison rather than a guess: no timesheet approval workflow, no automatic break deductions or custom pay periods, no smart reminders, alerts or automatic clock-out, no groups, one admin, two locations, one work schedule and one kiosk, and no attendance report, leave workflow or leave balances.

That puts the usual boundary exactly where you would expect it — approvals and team structure — while payroll export, overtime and the integrations stay free. A free plan that can export to your payroll system does remove work rather than move it; one that cannot approve a timesheet leaves that step with you. Second, note what stands where a price table would. An impact calculator projects your annual savings — for 100 people at $25 an hour it prints $270,400 — on three assumptions it publishes in small type: 40 hours a week, 48 working weeks, and 5.5% payroll leakage.

That last figure is theirs, not measured in your business. One thing that is a legal question rather than a pricing one: if you plan to use facial recognition for clock-ins, check your local rules on biometric data before you roll it out. That constraint does not appear on any tier.

What we found running Jibble ourselves

Hands-on · 2026-09-25

The first findings are at the top of this page. The rest:

  • Setting up the organisation requires a phone number; the form will not continue without one. The marketing opt-in on the sign-up form is ticked by default.
  • Clocking out in the same minute you clocked in is refused, with a message saying you cannot clock out before clocking in. Jibble records whole minutes; two minutes later it worked.
  • Inviting a second member took a name and an email address and went through without any prompt to pay. The member shows as pending until they accept.
  • The weekly timesheet export offers XLS or CSV. Our CSV arrived within seconds and carries 32 columns per member per day, including worked hours, paid and unpaid breaks, deductions, payroll hours, daily and weekly overtime and billable amounts.
  • We did not switch on face recognition or GPS. The comparison puts both in Free; anti-spoofing, device lock and live location tracking are Pro only.

How to actually use Jibble

Start on the free plan, because free forever with unlimited users is a real claim and an unusual one in a category where per-seat pricing is the norm. For a team on a tight budget that alone makes it worth an afternoon. Establish what the free tier excludes before you build a process on it. 'Unlimited users' and 'unlimited features' are different promises, and the paid tiers exist for a reason — ask which reports, approvals, integrations or policy controls sit above the line.

The answer is inside the app: the upgrade button opens Jibble's own plan comparison, one click further down. Read it before day 15, because a new organisation runs a 14-day trial of the paid features and nothing looks locked until that ends. Setting up the organisation also asks for a phone number, and the marketing opt-in on the sign-up form is ticked by default. Ignore the impact calculator and build your own number.

It now sits on the homepage where a price table used to be, and its output rests on three assumptions printed underneath it: 40 hours a week, 48 working weeks a year, and 5.5% payroll leakage. That is what a paid tier would have to beat.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is in the free plan?

Now there is a table rather than a slogan. The FAQ on jibble.io promises the software free for unlimited users, and inside the app, read 25 September 2026 in a new account, Jibble's own plan comparison draws the line. The short version: exports, overtime, biometric and GPS clock-ins and all the integrations are free; approvals and team structure are not. No timesheet approval workflow, no groups, one admin, two locations and one work schedule on Free. So you can run payroll from the free tier, but nobody signs a timesheet off inside Jibble unless you pay €1.79 per user a month on annual billing. One caution for testing it: a new organisation starts with a 14-day trial of the paid features, so run your trial pay cycle after day 14, on what Free actually keeps.

Can I use the face recognition and GPS tracking anywhere?

Technically yes, legally that is your question to answer before you switch them on. The product menu offers face recognition, biometric attendance, GPS time tracking with geofences, periodic screenshots and employee monitoring, and the free-for-unlimited-users promise makes them easy to roll out across a whole workforce in an afternoon. A face template is biometric data, and in the EU and UK that sits in a special category with consent, retention and impact-assessment duties attached; several US states regulate it separately. Location tracking of staff and screenshot capture carry their own rules and, in many countries, works-council or employee-representation obligations. None of that is an argument against the tool — it is an argument for deciding which of these features you actually need for payroll accuracy, and leaving the rest off. Ask your own adviser, not the vendor, and keep the answer with your records.

The calculator says I would save $270,400 a year. Is that a real number?

It is arithmetic, and the page is unusually honest about the arithmetic, which is what makes it useful. Read on 19 September 2026, the calculator sits where a price table used to be and prints its own assumptions underneath: 40 hours a week, 48 working weeks a year and 5.5% payroll leakage. On its default settings — 100 people at $25 an hour — it produces $270,400 a year, $22,533 a month and 260 recovered hours a week, against Jibble Free, which costs nothing. Every one of those figures is your payroll multiplied by their leakage assumption; nothing in it is measured in your business. The honest way to use it is to replace 5.5% with a number you can defend — the gap between clocked and scheduled hours in a month you have already closed — and see what survives. If you have never measured that gap, the calculator is telling you about Jibble's marketing, not your payroll.

What should I weigh before buying HR & People software?

Compliance is country by country, so the honest answer is: only where the vendor says it is, and only for the entity type you actually have. Ask which countries are covered by its own entities versus a local partner, who carries the liability if something is filed wrong, and how changes in local law reach you. Get those answers in the contract rather than the sales deck.
