What actually drives the quote

LettrLabs sells physical direct mail — handwritten letters and postcards produced by robots — so there is no subscription ladder to climb and the cost per piece is the whole question. Pricing is quoted rather than published, which for a print-and-postage product is normal: the number depends on volume, format and postage class in a way a tier table cannot express.

What to establish before the call, because these are the variables that move the quote: your monthly volume, whether you want handwritten letters or printed postcards, and whether postage is included in the per-piece price or added. Ask for the all-in landed cost per piece at your actual volume, not the print cost, because postage is a large and variable share of it.

The arithmetic that decides whether any of this works is on your side rather than theirs. Direct mail is priced per piece and judged on response rate, so the only number that matters is cost per acquired customer: pieces sent, times cost per piece, divided by customers won. At a one per cent response rate a $2 piece costs $200 per response before you have sold anything. Run that against your customer value before the first campaign, and start with a test volume small enough that a bad list costs you a lunch rather than a quarter.

The honest advantage of the format is that a handwritten envelope gets opened when email does not. The honest limit is that it cannot be iterated the way email can. A mailed piece is committed the moment it leaves, so the copy and the list have to be right before you spend — not after the first send tells you they were not.

How to actually use LettrLabs

Build the list before you think about the mail, because that is what decides whether any of this returns anything. Robotic handwriting gets the envelope opened — a real advantage over printed mail — but an opened envelope with the wrong name on it is still waste.

Then compute your break-even before the first drop. At around $0.49 a piece including postage, a thousand-piece drop is $490. Divide that by your gross margin per new customer: at a $200 margin you need three customers from a thousand pieces, which is a 0.3% response rate. Judge that against what your own channels actually produce, and you will know whether direct mail is a leverage channel for you or an expensive experiment.

Test the offer on a small drop first, and change one thing at a time. The handwriting is the delivery mechanism; the offer is what does the work. Send two hundred pieces with your best current offer before you commit to a thousand, and give it a trackable response route — a unique URL, a code, a dedicated number — because direct mail is worthless without attribution.

The natural comparison is Handwrytten — the main handwritten-mail rival. Decide by which one fits the job above, not by the louder brand.
