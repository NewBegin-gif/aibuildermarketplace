Pricing (2026)

✓ Pricing re-verified 19 Sep 2026

The page loads on annual billing, so the figures it shows first are the annual ones; both terms are given here. Basic is free with 3 hours of transcription a month. Pro $9.99 per user per month billed annually, $15.99 billed monthly, with 20 hours of transcription. Business $17 per user per month billed annually, $27 billed monthly, with unlimited transcription and team multi-meeting analytics.

Enterprise is quote-only: SSO, SCIM, org-wide settings, on-premise storage, custom retention, custom voice agents and a dedicated account manager. Extra transcription hours are $0.50 each. Downloads and exports and admin user management start on Pro, not Business; API and MCP access are listed on the free Basic plan. A 14-day free trial runs on Pro. Two limits sit beside the hours and neither is in the plan blurb.

A separate cap ends the individual meeting: two hours on Basic and Pro, three on Business, four on Enterprise. And your recordings expire on a schedule — transcripts are kept three months on Basic, a year on Pro and indefinitely above that, audio one month, six months and twelve, with video stored only from Business. On the same page, the annual badge claims up to 40% off, the actual step from monthly to annual is about 37% on both paid tiers, and a line under the heading offers 30% off for startups, non-profits and education on request.

Data can be stored in the EU or the US on every plan, the page claims SOC 2, GDPR and HIPAA compliance and states that customer data is not used to train external models, but recording-consent notifications with approval tracking start on Business. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

When the paid plan is worth it

Basic is free with three hours of transcription a month, Pro is $9.99 a user a month with twenty hours, and Business is $17 with unlimited transcription. Those two figures are the annual prices, which is what the pricing page shows before anyone touches the billing switch; paying monthly makes them $15.99 and $27. Extra hours are $0.50 each. Those numbers make the choice arithmetic rather than instinct, and the answer depends on the switch.

Twenty hours is about an hour a working day, and the overage closes the gap fast. On annual billing, Pro plus fourteen extra hours costs exactly what Business costs, so thirty-four hours a month is the line. On monthly billing the gap is wider — $15.99 against $27 — and the line moves to about forty-two hours. Below it Pro is cheaper; above it you are paying more than Business costs for less than Business gives.

Count the meetings you would actually record rather than the ones you attend, because that is where the estimate goes wrong. A sales rep on four calls a day passes twenty hours in the first week of the month. For that profile Pro is not a cheaper version of Business — it is a plan for occasional recording, and treating it as the budget option produces a bigger bill than the plan above it.

What Business adds beyond the meter is narrower than it looks. Team multi-meeting analytics is the feature that starts there; downloads and exports and admin user management are already on Pro, so a team that moves up for those is paying $17 for something it has at $9.99. Nor is the API a reason to climb: API and MCP access are listed on the free Basic plan. Enterprise is bought for governance instead — SSO, SCIM, org-wide settings, on-premise storage and custom retention.

There is a 14-day trial on Pro. One thing no tier settles: a recorder that joins every meeting changes what people say in them. Consent is a policy decision to make before the rollout, not a setting to find afterwards.

How to actually use MeetGeek

Settle consent before you switch it on, because a recorder that joins every meeting changes what people say in them — and in several jurisdictions it changes what is lawful. Write down who gets told, how they are told, and which meetings are excluded. This is a policy decision rather than a pricing one and it is the step most teams skip. Then count the meetings you would actually record, in hours.

Pro's twenty hours a month is about an hour a working day, which sounds generous until you apply it: a sales rep on four calls a day passes it in the first week. Overage runs $0.50 an hour, and Business with unlimited transcription becomes the cheaper plan past about 34 hours a month on annual billing ($9.99 against $17) or about 42 hours on monthly billing ($15.99 against $27) — so treat Pro as a plan for occasional recording rather than a cheaper version of the same thing.

Use the 14-day trial on your real meeting mix rather than a scheduled test call. Accents, crosstalk, a bad connection and someone dialling in from a car are what transcription accuracy actually has to survive, and every summary built on a poor transcript is confidently wrong.

Frequently asked questions

Which limit will I hit first — the hours or something else?

Probably the hours, but two other limits catch people out and neither is in the plan summary. The first is a cap on the single meeting: two hours on Basic and on Pro, three on Business, four on Enterprise. A monthly allowance of twenty hours does not help you through a three-hour workshop on Pro, and that is the sort of meeting you most want a record of. The second is storage, which expires by tier: transcripts are held three months on Basic, a year on Pro and without limit on Business, while audio lasts one month, six months and twelve, and video is only stored from Business at all. So a free account is not an archive — anything you want to keep past a quarter has to be exported, and downloads and transcript exports themselves start on Pro. Decide what you are buying: a note-taker for this week, or a searchable record you will still have next year.

What do I need for consent, and where does my data sit?

The compliance answers are good; the tooling that enforces them is tiered, and that is the part to plan around. Every plan lets you choose whether your data is stored in the EU or the US, and the page claims SOC 2, GDPR and HIPAA compliance and states that your meetings are not used to train external models. What arrives only on Business at $17 a user is the machinery for consent: recording-consent notifications with approval tracking, meetings that stay private by default, rules to skip meetings you do not want recorded, and a customisable recording disclaimer. On Pro you can still tell people you are recording — you just have to do it yourself, every time, and keep your own evidence. In a two-party-consent state, or for client calls under European rules, that difference is the difference between a policy you can show an auditor and a promise you made in a meeting. Price the tier by the policy you need, not only by the transcription hours.

Is the annual discount really 40%?

Close, but the badge rounds in its own favour, and the real answer is on the same page. Read 19 September 2026, the toggle promises up to 40% off for yearly billing. Work it through: Pro is $15.99 a user monthly against $9.99 on the annual term, which is 37.5% off, and Business is $27 against $17, which is 37%. So the discount is real, it is worth roughly $72 and $120 per user per year, and it is a little smaller than the sticker. The trade is the usual one — you commit for twelve months to a tool whose value depends on whether your team actually keeps using it after week three, so run the 14-day trial on your real meeting mix first. One more line worth knowing before you pay list price: the pricing page offers 30% off for startups, non-profits and education, on request rather than by button, and the worst answer you get by asking is no.

What should I weigh before buying Video & Audio software?

Judge it on evidence rather than adjectives. Look for a published security page, single sign-on and role controls on the plan you would actually buy (they are often gated to higher tiers), a status page with real incident history, and a clear answer on where your data is stored. If the tool will hold anything sensitive, those four answers matter more than any feature comparison.
