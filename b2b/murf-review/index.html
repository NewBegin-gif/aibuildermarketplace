Studio plans: Free $0 for 10 projects, 10 minutes of voice generation and one editor, with no downloads and no commercial rights. Enterprise is custom and is where voice cloning, SSO and AI translation sit. Annual billing is advertised as saving 33%. Dub and the API are separate products with their own pricing — the API tab of the pricing page meters by characters, not hours: $0.01 per 1,000 characters on the Falcon model and $0.03 on the studio-quality model, with $10 of free credit a month on the API trial and purchased credit that does not expire.

The Studio ladder on the page is Free, Creator, Business and Enterprise; there is no Growth plan, and Creator and Business are capped at one editor seat each, so a second person means Enterprise. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

What buyers report about Murf

Themes from the buyer-review sites listed at the foot of this page, read in August 2026. We have not run Murf ourselves, so this is other buyers' experience rather than ours. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • Voice quality is the consistent praise — 200+ voices across 30+ languages, with pitch, speed, pause and emphasis controllable per line.
  • • It has real traction with instructional designers and content teams who need clean narration without booking talent.
What they complain about
  • • The free tier is 10 minutes for the lifetime of the account, with no downloads. It is a demo, not a trial.
  • • Voice cloning — the reason many people look at Murf — is enterprise-only and needs a custom contract.
  • • Unused minutes do not roll over, and monthly billing costs about 33% more than annual.
  • • Refunds close after 24 hours or 10 minutes of use, whichever comes first.
  • • It is not built for real-time work. For an IVR or a live agent, latency becomes the ceiling.
  • • TrustRadius carries only one written Murf review, so treat it as a single voice — but it independently reaches the same two conclusions as the other platforms: no real-time or on-the-fly synthesis, so it is the wrong tool for live applications, and long texts process slowly. It adds one more: integration with other tools is thin.

When the paid plan is worth it

The split is unusually clear on Murf's side. The free workspace carries 10 minutes of voice generation time and a limit of 10 projects, and Murf states plainly that the free trial does not support downloads. That makes the free tier an audition sandbox rather than a production tool: anyone who needs a finished audio file has to pay, regardless of how little they generate. The paid tiers earn their keep when output is steady and predictable, because generation time is metered rather than unlimited.

The pricing page, read 14 September 2026, sells that time in yearly buckets: 24 hours on Creator and 96 on Business, which is about 2 and 8 hours a month, and only Enterprise is unmetered. Annual billing is where the economics shift, since Murf advertises a flat 33% off yearly subscriptions. Upgrading pointedly does not solve two things. It does not buy collaboration, because Murf documents that only the Enterprise workspace allows inviting users to collaborate on projects, so extra seats become a sales conversation rather than a checkbox.

And it does not cover API usage, which is billed separately on its own pay-as-you-go meter.

How to actually use Murf

The shortest route to something usable is to open a free workspace, paste a script into the Studio editor and generate it in sub-blocks rather than as one long block. Voice selection is not the place to economise: Murf documents that changing voice actor, style, pitch, speed, pause, emphasis, pronunciation, punctuation and volume for the same text will not consume any voice generation time. Editing the text is what bills.

The practical discipline is therefore to lock the script first, then experiment with delivery afterwards. Budget before generating. Murf estimates that a 1,000-word English script consumes roughly 6 minutes of voice generation time, so the free allowance of 10 minutes covers about one script and little rehearsal. That is also where most people hit the wall, because the free trial does not support downloads and the export step requires a paid plan.

Upgrading happens on the pricing page, where the Buy Now button is replaced by an Upgrade button that shows a pro-rated breakdown before anything is charged.

Frequently asked questions

What does Murf cost, and what do the hours mean?

Creator is $19 a month billed annually ($228 a year) and Business $66 a month ($792 a year), read on 17 August 2026 and unchanged on 3 October 2026. The unit that decides which you need is not projects but generation time: 24 hours a year on Creator, 96 on Business. That is an annual pool, not a monthly one, and Murf states unused time does not roll over, so a bursty production month is the case to plan for.

How does Murf compare with Synthesia?

They solve different halves of the same job and the comparison is one of the searches that brings people here. Murf is voice: 200+ voices across 30+ languages with per-line control over pitch, speed, pause and emphasis, priced by generation time. Synthesia is video with a presenter avatar. If you already have the visuals and need narration, Murf is the cheaper half; if you need a talking presenter on screen, Murf alone will not get you there.

Can I use Murf for dubbing or in a live application?

Dubbing is a separate Murf product with its own pricing, so a Studio subscription is not automatically a dubbing subscription — check which product you are buying. For live use, be careful: reviewers have consistently reported that it is not built for real-time synthesis, and latency is the ceiling for anything like an IVR or a live agent. The API, priced from $0.01 per 1,000 characters on the Falcon model, is the route for programmatic generation, and Murf now pitches Falcon at real-time voice agents, so test its latency yourself rather than relying on older reviews.

What should I weigh before buying Operations & Workflow software?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.
