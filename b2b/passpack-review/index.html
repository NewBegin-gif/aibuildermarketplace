When the paid plan is worth it

Teams is $20 per user per year, paid annually, for up to twenty people, and Business is $4.50 per user per month. Read on 25 August 2026 on their own pricing page. Teams works out at about $1.67 a user a month, which is genuinely a fraction of the mainstream managers where $3 to $8 a seat is normal — for a twenty-person team that is on the order of a thousand dollars a year.

The saving sits almost entirely in that one plan, though, and it is worth knowing before you grow into the next one. Business at $4.50 a seat is inside the normal range rather than under it, so past twenty people the price stops being the reason to choose this and the trade-offs below start doing all the work. What you trade for the price is polish, and that trade is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing.

The interface feels clunky and there are no native mobile apps, so on a phone you are in the browser. For a password manager that is not a cosmetic complaint: the tool only protects you if people actually use it, and friction on mobile is where team adoption quietly fails. A cheaper licence that half the team routes around is more expensive than a polished one they use. So the honest test during the 28-day trial is not the feature list but adoption: put it on the phones of the two least patient people on your team and see whether they still use it in week four.

If they do, the saving is real. If they do not, the mainstream alternative is cheaper than the breach that shared spreadsheet eventually causes.

How to actually use Passpack

Work out first whether you are under or over twenty people, because that is where the economics change. Teams is $20 per user per year for up to twenty; Business is $4.50 per user per month. That is a step of roughly 2.7 times per seat, so a team of eighteen and a team of twenty-two are looking at quite different propositions. Then use the 28-day trial to test the thing that actually decides adoption, which is the phone.

There are no native mobile apps, so on a phone you are in a browser — and a password manager people will not use on their phone is a password manager people will not use. Give it to the least technical person on your team and have them do a week of real logins from their handset before you commit anyone else. Be clear about the trade you are making.

You are buying solid zero-knowledge security at a fraction of the mainstream managers' price, and paying for it in polish: a clunky interface, no mobile apps, and reports of slow support and billing hiccups. For a team where cost matters more than convenience that can be exactly right — but it is a trade rather than a bargain, and the trial is where you find out which side you are on.

Plan the migration and the offboarding before you start. Check the import path from wherever your passwords live now, and check what happens to shared vaults when someone leaves — that second one is the reason teams buy a password manager in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

What does Passpack cost?

$20 per user per year for Teams, up to twenty people, and $4.50 per user per month for Business — both paid annually, both with a 28-day trial, read on 25 August 2026 on their own pricing page. Enterprise is a custom quote.

Is it really that much cheaper than the big password managers?

On Teams, yes: about $1.67 a user a month against the $3 to $8 a seat the mainstream business managers charge. On Business at $4.50 that gap largely closes, so the answer depends on which plan your headcount puts you in. What you weigh against that is the smaller ecosystem: fewer integrations and a smaller support organisation. For a team that mainly needs shared credentials with proper access control, that trade is often fine.

What should I test in the trial?

Twenty-eight days is generous, so use it on the two things that decide adoption: how credentials are shared and revoked when someone leaves, and whether the browser extension behaves on the sites your team actually uses. Password managers fail on those details rather than on features.

What should I weigh before buying IT & Productivity software?

Judge it on evidence rather than adjectives. Look for a published security page, single sign-on and role controls on the plan you would actually buy (they are often gated to higher tiers), a status page with real incident history, and a clear answer on where your data is stored. If the tool will hold anything sensitive, those four answers matter more than any feature comparison.
