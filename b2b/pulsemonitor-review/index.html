What PulseMonitor does

Uptime monitoring for developers and small teams: HTTP, API, port, keyword and SSL checks with incident history, plus a built-in SEO site audit; free plan with 50 monitors and a paid plan for 1-minute checks, webhooks, status pages and heartbeats.

Free watches, Pro wakes you up

Free is a real monitoring plan, not a teaser: 50 monitors is more than most side projects and small apps need, and HTTP, port, keyword, SSL and the SEO analyzer are all included. Its limits are speed and routing. A 5-minute interval means an outage can run up to five minutes before the first check sees it, and alerts go to email, Discord or Telegram only. Pro at $9 a month is about the things around the check.

One-minute polling, webhooks (the pricing page names Slack and PagerDuty as the reason to want them), escalation, status pages for customers, heartbeats that notice a cron job that silently stopped, ping and DNS monitors and JSON validation of API responses are all Pro-only. Retention goes from 3 to 24 months. Yearly billing at $90 against $108 for twelve months is a saving of $18, our arithmetic. Weigh it against the refund policy: annual fees are non-refundable, so a year paid upfront is a year committed.

The documentation narrows what the Slack and PagerDuty promise means. Discord and Telegram alerts work on both plans; Slack, Microsoft Teams, Zapier, PagerDuty and custom webhooks are Pro-only. PagerDuty has no native connector: the PagerDuty guide recommends sending PulseMonitor's webhook through Zapier, or running a small proxy of your own that turns it into a PagerDuty Events API v2 call, because PulseMonitor currently sends a generic webhook format. An on-call team on PagerDuty should budget a Zapier plan or a few lines of code on top of the $9.

The API is the other Pro-only lever. It lists, creates and deletes monitors and returns checks and incidents, with a Bearer token that is shown only once and a limit of 60 requests per minute per token. An AI agent working for you needs that same token: PulseMonitor's auth.md says there is no automated or anonymous agent registration, only a token generated by a verified-email account on Pro. An OpenAPI description is published at /openapi.json.

    How to actually use PulseMonitor

    Start on Free with your three most important URLs: the home page, a health endpoint of your API and the checkout or login page. Add a keyword check on a page whose content matters, because a page can return 200 while showing an error message, and keyword monitoring is on the free plan. Set up alerts in the channel your team actually reads. On Free that is email, Discord or Telegram; if your on-call lives in Slack or PagerDuty you will need Pro's webhooks.

    Run the SEO analyzer once on your own site to see whether that half of the product is useful to you, since it is part of the reason to pick PulseMonitor over a pure uptime tool. Only move to Pro when you can name the trigger: one-minute detection, a public status page, or a cron job that must not fail silently. Pay monthly first; the refund policy gives no refund on a year you no longer want.

    For a cron job on Pro, create a heartbeat under Heartbeats, choose how often you expect a ping (the docs use every 15 minutes or every hour as examples) and add a POST to the ping URL at the end of the job, for instance after a nightly backup script. The token in that URL is the only authentication, so treat the URL as a secret. When a ping does not arrive in time, the heartbeat docs describe an email titled 'Heartbeat missed'; they mention no other channel for heartbeats.

    For a public uptime figure, the badge docs describe two SVG badges, 30-day uptime and 30-day average response time, that load without a login, are cached for 5 minutes, cannot show any period other than 30 days and carry a 'Powered by PulseMonitor' line that PulseMonitor asks you to link to its home page. Unlike the API, heartbeat and webhook pages, the badge page does not mark badges as Pro-only.

    The one-off checker tools on the website are not a stand-in for an account: the terms limit guests to 3 checks per hour per tool.

    Is PulseMonitor free?

    Yes, read on 14 October 2026: the Free plan includes 50 monitors at a 5-minute interval, HTTP, port and keyword checks, SSL expiry checks, email, Discord and Telegram alerts, the SEO analyzer and 3 months of history, with no credit card. Pro is $9 a month or $90 a year.

    Can PulseMonitor alert Slack or PagerDuty?

    Through webhooks, which are Pro-only. The pricing page itself says to reach for Pro when alerts must hit Slack or PagerDuty. The free plan alerts by email, Discord and Telegram. Read on 14 October 2026.

    Can I get a refund from PulseMonitor?

    Generally not. The refund policy, last updated 14 October 2026, says monthly and annual fees are non-refundable and there is no prorated refund on cancellation. Refunds are at their discretion for billing errors, extended outages you reported promptly, or where the law in your country requires one.

    Does PulseMonitor connect to PagerDuty directly?

    Not natively. The PagerDuty guide (read 25 October 2026) says the integration works through a webhook on Pro, and recommends Zapier: a Catch Hook trigger in Zapier, PulseMonitor's Zapier integration pointed at it, and a PagerDuty Create Incident action. Calling PagerDuty's Events API v2 directly needs a proxy of your own, because PulseMonitor sends a generic webhook with only a text field describing the down or recovered event. Resolving incidents on recovery may need a second Zap.

    Can an AI agent manage PulseMonitor monitors for me?

    Yes, through the REST API on Pro. PulseMonitor's auth.md (read 25 October 2026) says an agent uses an API token that the account owner generates under Settings, API, after registering with a verified email and upgrading to Pro; there is no automated or anonymous agent registration. The token goes in a Bearer header, is shown once, and is limited to 60 requests per minute. The API can list, create and delete monitors and read checks and incidents; an OpenAPI file is at pulsemonitor.io/openapi.json.

    What happens to my monitoring history if I stop paying?

    The pricing page gives 24 months of data retention on Pro and 3 months on Free, but does not say what happens to Pro history older than 3 months after a downgrade. The terms say that after termination PulseMonitor has no obligation to keep your data and that exporting what you need is your responsibility. Paid access runs to the end of the period you paid for, without a refund for the rest.

    Who runs PulseMonitor?

    The pages do not say. The terms, refund policy and privacy policy name only PulseMonitor, and the terms are governed by the laws of the jurisdiction in which we operate, without naming it. What is published is a security contact (security@pulsemonitor.io, in an RFC 9116 security.txt valid to October 2027) and, in humans.txt, that the service is built with Laravel, Livewire and MaryUI. The privacy policy says data may be processed outside your country, with safeguards for transfers from the EEA and UK.
