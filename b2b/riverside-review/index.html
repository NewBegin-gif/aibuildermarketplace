What Riverside does

Records podcasts and video locally at each end, so guests on poor connections still come out broadcast-quality. The meter is separate-track recording time: Pro is $29/month for 15 hours, and hitting the cap stops new separate-track downloads until the next cycle rather than billing you more.

The honest knock

Their own wording on hitting the cap: you can still reach your content but cannot download new separate tracks until the next billing cycle or you upgrade. So a busy interview month does not cost you more money, it quietly stops you delivering, which is worse. Fifteen hours on Pro is about seven or eight hour-long interviews once you count retakes and the recording that runs while you set up.

The free tier is a demonstration rather than a plan: one 2-hour session, 720p, single track.

Grow moves the wall 33% for $10

The meter here is not recording time, it is separate-track recording time — and separate tracks are the entire reason to use Riverside rather than a video call. Read on their own pricing page on 1 August 2026: Free is one 2-hour recording at 720p, single track, 44.1 kHz. Pro is $29 a month or $24 billed annually for 15 hours a month, up to 4K, 48 kHz, one studio.

Grow is $39 ($34 annually) for 20 hours and two studios. Webinar is $99 ($79 annually) for 25 hours and three studios. Business is quote-only with unlimited hours. Per hour, Pro is the cheapest plan on the ladder at $1.60, Grow is $1.70 and Webinar is $3.16. So the volume discount runs backwards, and if hours were all you were buying, nobody would ever leave Pro. The marginal figures say the same thing louder: Grow's five extra hours cost $2.00 each, Webinar's five cost $9.00 each.

The second studio costs $10; the third costs $45. Which reframes the decision. What you are buying when you leave Pro is not hours, it is distance from the wall — and the wall is unusual in that hitting it does not cost you money. Riverside's own wording is that you can still reach your content but cannot download new separate tracks until the next billing cycle or you upgrade.

A busy interview month therefore does not produce a bigger invoice, it produces a stop: the recordings exist and you cannot deliver them. For anyone with a publishing schedule that is the expensive outcome. Fifteen hours is fewer sessions than it sounds. Count the recording that runs while you set up levels, the retakes and the overrun, and seven or eight hour-long interviews is a realistic month on Pro. If your schedule is anywhere near that, Grow's $10 moves the ceiling out by a third and is the cheapest insurance on this page.

The AI credits are a separate meter — 20 on Pro and Grow, from 60 on Business, and more are bought on top — so do not let them influence the tier choice. Treat the free plan as a demonstration rather than a plan. One two-hour session at 720p on a single track is enough to see the interface and nothing like enough to test the thing you are paying for.

How to actually use Riverside

Record one real session on the free plan before anything else, and pay attention to the upload rather than the recording. Riverside's value is that each participant is captured locally and uploaded separately, so the failure mode that matters is a guest with a poor connection or a browser that misbehaves — test that with a guest, not with yourself. Then measure your hours the way the meter does. It counts separate-track recording time, which includes the minutes running while you set levels, every retake, and the overrun at the end.

Add those to your interview length and the honest monthly figure is usually half again what the schedule suggests. Pick the tier from that number and from your publishing calendar, not from the price. Hitting the cap does not cost money — it stops you downloading new separate tracks until the cycle turns over — so the risk you are buying out of is a missed delivery date. If your estimate lands anywhere near Pro's 15 hours, Grow's extra five cost $10 and are worth it purely as headroom.

The natural comparison is Zencastr or SquadCast — the direct local-recording peers — compare the monthly hour allowance for separate tracks, not the headline price. Weigh it against Zencastr on the job you actually need done.
