What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Nothing here invents a number. But the visitors counter carries two settings that shape which true number a shopper sees. It can show the counter only after a set number of views, so a quiet product simply displays nothing rather than a small figure. And the time frame is yours to set, so 'twelve people viewed this' can mean twelve in five minutes or twelve since yesterday, presented identically. Neither setting fabricates anything; together they let you present a true number that a reasonable shopper would read as something else.

That distinction is where the legal line sits. The FTC treats false urgency and scarcity as deceptive practice under section 5 of the FTC Act, and the examples in its dark-patterns guidance are close to these mechanics: a low-stock claim when stock is plentiful, or a cart-activity figure well above the real one. The test is whether a reasonable consumer would be misled about a material fact. That test does not apply to the app; it applies to how you configure it, which means the compliance question lands on your desk rather than the developer's.

When the paid plan is worth it

Bought together these three cost under seven dollars a month, so the money is not the decision. The decision is which of them tells your shopper something true. The low stock counter is the easiest of the three to run honestly, because it reads your inventory. If it says two are left, two are left. The setting to watch is the threshold: choose a number that reflects genuine scarcity for your product rather than one that keeps the badge permanently lit, because a badge that always says 'low stock' stops being information and becomes decoration.

The visitors counter is where the paid step is smallest and the care needed is largest. Its $1.99 tier adds a most-viewed-product upsell banner, which is a straightforward merchandising feature. The free tier is where the judgement sits, and the two settings that matter are covered in the knock below. The countdown timer is listed free, and its own description is the clearest statement of intent on any of the three: build urgency from homepage to checkout, and a cart timer that reserves items to force buyers to act fast.

If the reservation is real, that is a service. If the timer resets when the shopper reloads, it is theatre, and that is the thing to test before you turn it on.

How to actually use Scarcity.AI

Decide what is true before you decide what to display. The low stock counter reads your inventory, so the only real setting is the threshold: pick a number that means something for your product rather than one that keeps the badge lit permanently. A badge that is always on stops being read. On the visitors counter, set the time frame to the window a shopper would assume. Most people reading 'nine people are viewing this' assume now, not this week.

If the honest window produces an unimpressive number, that is information about your traffic rather than a reason to widen it, and the 'show only after X views' setting is the same decision in another form. Test the countdown timer by reloading the page. A cart timer that claims to reserve your items should behave the same way on a refresh and in a new session; if the clock starts over, it is not reserving anything and the claim on the page is not true.

Do that test before the timer goes anywhere near a live product page. Each app installs and bills separately, so start with the one that matches something genuinely true about your store, and add the others only if the same is true of them.
