Below fifteen staff you pay for fifteen

Shiftie charges per active employee per month — roughly £2.75, dropping to about £1.50 once you pass around 30 staff — with entry plans from about £39 a month and a 28-day free trial on everything (2026). That £39 floor is the number that decides most small deployments. Divide it by £2.75 and it covers about fourteen employees, which means a team below that size is paying for people it does not have.

A five-person café pays £39, or £7.80 a head — 2.8 times the advertised rate. The headline £2.75 only becomes your real rate at roughly fifteen staff, and everything below that is a minimum dressed as a per-person price. The second rate deserves a direct question before you sign, because how it is applied changes the bill by half. If the £1.50 replaces £2.75 for everyone once you pass 30 staff, a 50-person team pays £75 a month.

If it applies only to the employees above 30, the same team pays £112.50. That is £450 a year on a difference the pricing description does not settle — ask which it is, in writing, and get the answer against your own headcount rather than in general. Then there is the seasonal edge of the active-employee model, which cuts both ways honestly. You only pay for active users, so a quiet winter genuinely costs less.

But a pub that runs twelve staff in January and thirty-five in July pays the £39 floor in winter and about £96 in summer — so the annual average is not the headline, and budgeting from a quiet month will surprise you. The 28-day trial is worth naming as a real advantage. Most software gives you fourteen days; rota software needs a complete cycle plus the week where someone calls in sick and a swap has to happen at short notice.

Twenty-eight days is long enough to hit that, which is the only test that matters. What you are buying is focused — scheduling, clock-ins, leave and swaps, done well — and not a payroll or HR platform, so a growing business will still bolt those on around it.

How to actually use Shiftie

Use all 28 days and build a real rota, not a sample one. The trial is twice the usual length, and the reason to use the extra fortnight is that scheduling software fails at the edges. Someone calls in sick. A swap has to be approved on a phone at seven in the morning, a shift crosses midnight, somebody's leave overlaps a busy weekend. None of that happens in week one.

Work out your real monthly cost before the trial ends. If you have fewer than about fifteen active staff, the £39 entry floor is your price rather than the £2.75 per head — a five-person team pays £7.80 a head in practice. If you have more than 30, ask in writing whether the reduced £1.50 rate applies to everyone or only to staff above 30, because at 50 employees that is the difference between £75 and £112.50 a month.

If your headcount swings with the season, model your busiest month rather than the average. You only pay for active employees, which genuinely saves money in the quiet months, but it also means summer costs what summer costs.

Frequently asked questions

What should I weigh before buying Operations & Workflow software?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.
