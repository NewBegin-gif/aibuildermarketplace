Pricing (2026)

✓ Pricing re-verified 19 Sep 2026

TheTop publishes no price. The navigation carries no pricing link, every button is Book a demo, and the page this review's figures came from is gone: thetop.com/pricing returns a hard 404, as do /pricing/ and /plans. The $8 a month and $80 a year this review used to quote were withdrawn along with the product they belonged to, and cannot be bought today.

Until September this was a personal assistant on a single tier, built around a morning brief from Gmail and a calendar. TheTop is now enterprise software sold through a sales call. Read the figures on their site carefully, because it is full of dollar amounts that are not prices: the dashboard mock-ups show an AI invoice of $196,090, a month billed at $137,644 against a $176,920 baseline, and $39,276 saved.

Those illustrate what the product reports, not what it costs. Nothing on the public site says what you would pay for TheTop itself. What the site does commit to about commercial shape: deployment is single-tenant, one dedicated instance per customer; onboarding runs in three steps that start read-only (connect analytics, watch for about a month to establish your number, then point traffic at the Engine); and no traffic migration is needed on day one.

Expect a contract quoted against your own AI spend rather than a list price — ask for the pricing basis in writing before the demo, because a layer that bills against measured savings and one that bills a flat platform fee are very different deals. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

The honest knock

TheTop publishes no rate of any kind: no list price, no per-seat figure, no percentage, and no pricing page — we checked on 19 September 2026 and the old one returns a 404. Everything runs through Book a demo, so the first number you hear will be quoted to you, with your own AI spend already on the table. That is a weak position to negotiate from, and it is worth asking for the basis (flat platform fee, per request, or a share of measured savings) before you show anyone your invoice.

The savings claim is measured by the vendor, against a baseline the vendor computes. The site says eligible traffic is priced against your own baseline and shows 22% and 43% saved in its examples, but the party doing the measuring is also the party being paid. Ask how baseline is defined, which traffic counts as eligible, and whether you can audit the calculation — a reuse or reroute that quietly swaps a frontier model for a cheaper one also changes the output, and only your own evaluation will tell you whether that trade was worth it.

One more thing to weigh: this product sits in the request path. The Engine applies memory, caching, routing and policy before traffic reaches the model, which is exactly where the savings come from and also exactly where an outage or a bad routing rule lands on every AI feature you run. The vendor's answer is that you start read-only and move traffic when you choose, which is the right shape — but the day you point production at it, a middle layer has become a dependency.

What TheTop does

An enterprise layer in front of your AI providers: it attributes spend by department, reuses repeated work, routes each task to a cheaper model and applies policy before execution. Sold by demo only — no published price since the pricing page went.

There is no upper tier to weigh

There is nothing to compare, because no tier is priced. TheTop sells one thing — the intelligence layer — and sells it by demo, so the question is not which plan to take but what the contract does to your AI bill. The shape the site describes: single-tenant deployment per customer, a read-only dashboard first, and the Engine switched on afterwards against an endpoint (engine.thetop.com/v1) that replaces your provider base URL without code changes.

That means the first phase costs you access and time rather than migration work, and the second phase is the one with real operational weight. What you should price it against is what it claims to save. The vendor's own examples put savings at 22% to 43% of eligible traffic, so a business spending six figures a year on model APIs is being sold a share of a five-figure number.

If the quote is a flat fee, the arithmetic is simple. If it is a share of measured savings, the definition of the baseline is the whole negotiation, and it should be in the contract rather than in the dashboard.

How to actually use TheTop

There is no signup and no trial to click; the first step is a form and a call. The site asks for company name, work email and which AI providers you use, then puts you with a specialist for a walk-through on traffic like your own. Their stated sequence is worth holding them to, because it is the low-risk one. First, connect read-only access and run the dashboard to see how teams actually use AI — no traffic moves.

Second, watch it for about a month, which is how long the vendor says it takes before you know your own savings number. Only then do you point traffic at the Engine by swapping the base URL to engine.thetop.com/v1, which the site says needs nothing rewritten on your side. Use the read-only month as the actual evaluation. It is the phase where you can check their baseline against your own invoices before any request depends on them, and it is the only leverage you will have on price.

Go into it with two things written down: what you currently spend per team, and what you would accept as proof that a reroute did not degrade output quality.

What should I weigh before buying IT & Productivity software?

Judge it on evidence rather than adjectives.
