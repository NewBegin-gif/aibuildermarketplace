What TicketTailor does

Low-fee event ticketing and box-office software: sell tickets online, manage check-in and keep more of your revenue without per-ticket booking fees.

Pricing (2026)

(2026-08-15, read on their own pricing page) No subscription and no contract: pay-as-you-sell $0.85/ticket, or prepaid credits from $0.30/ticket — 500 credits cost $300, which is $0.60 each. Free events are genuinely free up to 5,000 tickets a year, your first five paid tickets cost nothing, and charities, B Corps, PTAs and not-for-profits get 50% off, as do very low-priced tickets. Seat reservations cost an extra credit per reserved seat.

Prices switch by currency (GBP, EUR, USD, AUD, CAD) — these are the US ones. Stripe, PayPal or Square processing fees apply separately and Ticket Tailor never touches the payment. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

The honest knock

The fee honesty is the product — flat cents per ticket instead of percentage skims makes it dramatically cheaper than the big platforms on higher-priced tickets (a $0.30-0.85 fee on a $50 ticket vs a percentage is no contest). The trade: you bring your own audience. There's no discovery marketplace pushing your event, so if you rely on a platform's browsing traffic to sell out, the cheap fees won't fill seats.

The flat fee wins as ticket prices rise

No subscription and no contract: you pay $0.85 a ticket as you sell, or buy prepaid credits from $0.30 a ticket — 500 credits cost $300, which is $0.60 each. Free events are genuinely free up to 5,000 tickets a year, your first five paid tickets cost nothing, and charities, B Corps, PTAs and not-for-profits get 50% off, as do very low-priced tickets. Prices switch by currency and these are the US ones (2026-08-15, read on their own pricing page). The credit ladder is worth doing. Five hundred credits at $300 is 29% below the pay-as-you-sell rate, and the top of the ladder at $0.30 is 65% below it. Against pay-as-you-sell, 500 credits saves $125 over 500 tickets. The one thing to confirm before prepaying is whether credits expire — the page we read does not say, and that single answer decides whether buying ahead is a saving or a bet. Now the comparison that actually matters, because it is why this model exists. A flat fee behaves completely differently from a percentage as ticket prices change. At $0.85 a ticket you are paying 1.7% on a $50 ticket, 8.5% on a $10 ticket and 17% on a $5 one. So the advantage over percentage-taking platforms is enormous at the top and disappears at the bottom — which is exactly why very low-priced tickets get 50% off here. To find your own crossover, divide your per-ticket fee by the percentage your current platform charges: at $0.60 a credit against a hypothetical 5% platform, the two cost the same at a $12 ticket, and above that the flat fee wins by more with every pound you add to the price. One line to add to the model if you have a seated venue: seat reservations cost an extra credit per reserved seat, so budget two units per ticket rather than one. For a 400-seat theatre that doubles your platform cost. There is a structural advantage that never appears as a discount. You connect your own Stripe, PayPal or Square account and Ticket Tailor never touches the payment — so the money arrives on your processor's normal schedule rather than on a platform's payout timetable. For an organiser paying deposits before the doors open, cash arriving weeks earlier is worth more than a few cents a ticket. The trade is honest and it is the whole risk: you bring your own audience. There is no discovery marketplace pushing your event at anyone, so if you have been relying on a platform's browsing traffic to fill the last hundred seats, cheap fees will not do that job. Count how many of your last event's sales came from the platform itself rather than from your own email list and social channels before you switch.

How to actually use TicketTailor

Work out where your sales came from last time before you switch. Ticket Tailor has no discovery marketplace, so every sale has to come from your own list, your own social channels or your own posters. If a meaningful share of your last event was filled by people browsing a platform, that is the traffic you are giving up and no fee saving replaces it. Then compare fees on your actual ticket price, not in the abstract. A flat $0.85 is 1.7% of a $50 ticket and 17% of a $5 one — divide your per-ticket fee by your current platform's percentage to find the price at which they cost the same. And if you run a seated venue, budget two units per ticket: seat reservations cost an extra credit each. Start on pay-as-you-sell and ask one question before you prepay: do credits expire? Five hundred credits at $300 saves $125 against the $0.85 rate, which is worth having — but only if the expiry terms suit an organiser who runs three events a year. Check too whether you qualify for the 50% charity, B Corp, PTA or not-for-profit rate, and connect your own Stripe, PayPal or Square account so the money reaches you on your processor's schedule.

Best for: event organizers with their own audience (venues, communities, creators) who want flat low per-ticket fees, full branding and no subscription.
Who should skip it: if you depend on marketplace discovery to sell tickets (Eventbrite's traffic is the fee you're paying for), or you run two events a year at tiny volume.

The natural comparison is Eventbrite or Humanitix — Eventbrite trades higher percentage fees for discovery traffic; Humanitix routes fees to charity — Ticket Tailor wins on raw cost per ticket. Weigh it against Eventbrite on the job you actually need done.
