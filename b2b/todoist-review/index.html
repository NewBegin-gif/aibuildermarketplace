Todoist Review (2026)The Free Plan Has No Reminders

What Todoist does

Task manager for individuals and small teams: quick capture, due dates and projects across devices. The free plan caps you at five projects and has no reminders, which moves most regular users to Pro at about $5/month billed annually. A to-do app, not a project suite with timelines or dependencies.

Pricing (2026)

Free (up to 5 projects, no reminders); Pro about $5/mo (annual, ~$7 monthly), Business about $8/user/mo (annual, ~$10 monthly) (2026). Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

The free plan has no reminders, which is the point

Free covers up to 5 projects and has no reminders. Pro is about $5 a month on annual billing (around $7 monthly) and Business about $8 per user a month annually (around $10 monthly) — so $60 and $96 a year respectively, with a 40% surcharge for paying monthly on Pro and 25% on Business (2026). The missing reminders are the honest heart of the free plan, and worth naming plainly rather than treating as one feature among several. A task manager without reminders is a list: it can hold what you decided to do, but it cannot tell you when. If everything you track is time-bound — client deadlines, renewals, a call at four — the free tier structurally cannot do the job you downloaded it for, and no amount of discipline substitutes. That is not a criticism of the pricing; it is the thing to know before you spend a month building a system on it. The five-project cap does the rest. Most people organise by area of life rather than by project — work, home, a side venture, errands, somebody else's admin — and five goes quickly. Between the cap and the reminders, the free plan is best understood as a generous trial rather than a permanent home. Which is fine, because Pro is genuinely cheap. Sixty dollars a year for something you open several times a day is not a decision that needs a spreadsheet, and the honest question is not whether it is worth the money but whether you will still be using it in six months — which is a question about habits, not about software. Take the monthly rate for the first quarter if you are unsure; $2 a month is a cheap way to find out. Business at $8 a user adds the team layer. The step from $5 to $8 is 60% per person, and it is worth it only if you actually assign work to other people rather than merely wanting them to see your list. Know the boundary in both directions. This is a personal and small-team task manager, and it is very good at that. It is not a project platform: no dependencies, no critical path, nothing Gantt-shaped. If you need to know that slipping task B moves the launch date, you need a different category of tool, and no Todoist tier becomes one.

How to actually use Todoist

Decide first whether reminders are part of what you need, because the free plan does not have them. If the things you track have times attached — deadlines, calls, renewals — the free tier cannot do the job and a month spent building a system on it is a month wasted. If you genuinely just want a list you check on purpose, free is fine indefinitely. Set up your projects by area of life rather than by task, and see how fast you reach five. Work, home, a side project and errands is already four for most people, so the cap tends to bind sooner than it sounds. If you upgrade, take Pro monthly for the first quarter rather than annually. The difference is about $2 a month, and what you are buying with it is the option to walk away once you find out whether the habit stuck — which is the only real risk at this price. Move to Business only if you actually assign work to other people; at $8 a user it is 60% more per head, and shared visibility alone is not worth that.

Best for: individuals and small teams who want a fast, elegant task manager that gets out of the way.
Who should skip it: if you need full project management with timelines, workloads and dependencies — this is a to-do app, not a PM suite.

The natural comparison is TickTick or Microsoft To Do — other task managers. Weigh it against Asana on the job you actually need done.

Frequently asked questions

Does Todoist replace my current setup or sit on top of it?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.

Who should not use Todoist?

Skip it if you need full project management with timelines, workloads and dependencies — this is a to-do app, not a PM suite. Buying a tool to fix a problem you don't have yet just adds cost and another login to manage.
