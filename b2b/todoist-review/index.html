The free plan reminds you when a task is due, never before

Free covers up to 5 projects and gives every scheduled task one automatic reminder, which fires at the moment the task comes due. Pro is about $5 a month on annual billing (around $7 monthly) and Business about $8 per user a month annually (around $10 monthly). That is $60 and $96 a year respectively, with a 40% surcharge for paying monthly on Pro and 25% on Business (2026). The reminder limit is about timing rather than absence, and that distinction is the whole upgrade case.

Todoist's own documentation splits reminders into four kinds — automatic, custom, recurring and location. Free gets the automatic one: schedule a task for four o'clock and it tells you at four o'clock. What Pro buys is the interval before, a custom reminder set thirty minutes ahead of the call or the morning before the renewal, and more than one of them on the same task. If you act on things at the moment they surface, the free tier genuinely does the job.

If you need warning — prep time, a document to read first, a decision to make before the deadline rather than at it — that is the feature you are paying for, and no amount of discipline substitutes for it. The five-project cap does the rest. Most people organise by area of life rather than by project — work, home, a side venture, errands, somebody else's admin — and five goes quickly.

Between the cap and the reminder timing, the free plan is best understood as a generous trial rather than a permanent home. Which is fine, because Pro is genuinely cheap. Sixty dollars a year for something you open several times a day is not a decision that needs a spreadsheet. The honest question is not whether it is worth the money, but whether you will still be using it in six months.

That is a question about habits, not about software. Take the monthly rate for the first quarter if you are unsure; $2 a month is a cheap way to find out. Business at $8 a user adds the team layer. The step from $5 to $8 is 60% per person, and it is worth it only if you actually assign work to other people rather than merely wanting them to see your list.

Know the boundary in both directions. This is a personal and small-team task manager, and it is very good at that. It is not a project platform: no dependencies, no critical path, nothing Gantt-shaped. If you need to know that slipping task B moves the launch date, you need a different category of tool, and no Todoist tier becomes one.

How to actually use Todoist

Decide first whether you need warning or only notice, because that is the line the free plan draws. Schedule a task with a time and free reminds you when it comes due; a reminder set for some interval before — thirty minutes ahead of a call, the morning before a renewal — is a Pro feature, as is putting more than one on the same task. If you act on things at the moment they surface, free is fine indefinitely.

If you need lead time, find that out in week one rather than after a month of building a system on it. Set up your projects by area of life rather than by task, and see how fast you reach five. Work, home, a side project and errands is already four for most people, so the cap tends to bind sooner than it sounds. If you upgrade, take Pro monthly for the first quarter rather than annually.

The difference is about $2 a month, and what you are buying with it is the option to walk away once you find out whether the habit stuck — which is the only real risk at this price. Move to Business only if you actually assign work to other people; at $8 a user it is 60% more per head, and shared visibility alone is not worth that.

Frequently asked questions

Is paying monthly for Todoist Pro much more expensive?

It comes to $24 a year more. Read 25 September 2026 with the page set to USD: Pro is $5 a month on annual billing, charged as $60 up front, or $7 on monthly billing, which is $84 over twelve months. Business is $8 per user on annual billing ($96) against $10 monthly ($120), plus local tax on both. Monthly costs more but binds you for one month at a time, which makes it the sensible way to find out whether the habit sticks before paying for a year.

What exactly does the free plan cap?

Five personal projects, three filter views, 5 MB per uploaded file and one week of activity history (plan comparison on the pricing page, read 25 September 2026). Archived projects do not count towards the project limit, according to the page's own tooltip, so finished work can be parked instead of deleted. Pro lifts those limits to 300 projects, 150 filters, 100 MB uploads and full history, and adds the calendar layout, task duration, deadlines and custom reminders. Free also gets limited Ramble sessions where Pro and Business get unlimited ones.

Can I share projects with other people without paying for Business?

Yes, within limits. The comparison table (read 25 September 2026) allows 5 people per personal project on every plan, the free one included. Business adds something different: a shared team workspace with up to 500 team projects, 250 people per team project, up to 1,000 members and guests, team roles and permissions, and centralised billing. A team created without paying gets 5 projects, per the page's tooltip. A household list shared by three people fits in personal-project sharing; permissions and one invoice for a team are what Business is for.

What should I weigh before buying Operations & Workflow software?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.
