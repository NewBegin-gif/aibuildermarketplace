What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

You pay the Swiss-E2EE premium — noticeably above mainstream clouds and above Proton Drive per GB — and the encryption trade-offs are inherent: no server-side content search, more deliberate sharing flows, and credential/recovery discipline is on you. For regulated teams that's exactly the deal; for casual storage it's over-engineering.

What buyers report about Tresorit

Recurring themes from G2, Cloudwards, Capterra and the Apple App Store, read in August 2026 (App Store reviews through July 2026, Capterra review text through April 2025). We have not run Tresorit ourselves, so this is other buyers' experience rather than ours. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • End-to-end, zero-knowledge encryption that is actually implemented properly — the 2024 ETH Zurich study found real vulnerabilities in competitors' implementations and not in Tresorit's.
  • • For regulated work in healthcare, legal or finance, reviewers treat it as the default choice.
  • • App Store reviewers name client-side encryption before upload as the specific reason they put financial and estate documents here rather than on a service that only encrypts in transit, and Swiss and EU storage as the reason they left Dropbox, OneDrive or Proton Drive.
  • • On Capterra, accountants, financial advisers and doctors describe letting clients without a Tresorit account receive files securely, with an audit trail of who opened what. A marketing consultant names GDPR compliance as the reason EU clients required it over Google Drive.
What they complain about
  • • Price is the top complaint on G2 by a wide margin — the "expensive" tag appears dozens of times, including from reviewers who rate the security highly. Re-read on 14 August 2026, the business plans are Professional, Business and Business Pro, and from our location they price in euros: €23.99, €16 and €20 per user per month on yearly billing, with minimums of 1, 3 and 5 users.
  • • Large files sync slowly and file size limits are low.
  • • No real-time collaboration, so it will not replace your daily working drive.
  • • No password recovery. That is the price of zero-knowledge, but it is a real operational risk — plan for it.
  • • Two Capterra complaints are about money rather than encryption. A self-employed CEO paid €142 for an annual subscription, could not open the app on either Windows or iPhone, got no working fix in two months and was then refused a refund because the eight-day window had passed (April 2025). Another sole trader was billed for a full year after a free trial without advance notice and got no answer when escalating (December 2024).
  • • Support is described on both platforms as slow or absent — one App Store reviewer waited weeks after a sync failure before switching away.
  • • Practical friction on the Mac: one long-term Capterra reviewer says the Mac client does not sync as a real local drive the way the Windows one does, so files must be downloaded, edited and re-uploaded. Photo and video uploads are reported stalling or duplicating.
  • • One App Store reviewer lost 90 GB of photos and video after a crash with no way to recover them, and another reports camera backup stopping without warning and not being restorable retroactively.

Where the ratings disagree. Capterra and the App Store surface a category of complaint that the security-focused reviews on G2 and Cloudwards do not: refused refunds, silent annual renewal and unanswered support tickets. The encryption is not what people complain about — the billing and the support are. Read the two sets together rather than choosing one: the product does what it claims, and the commercial relationship is where buyers report getting hurt.

When the paid plan is worth it

The free Basic account is permanent rather than a countdown, but its ceilings are low: 3 GB of encrypted storage, uploads capped at 500 MB per file, syncing across 2 devices, and no more than 10 encrypted share links a month. That covers a private archive of sensitive documents and little else. Paying starts to make sense the moment file size or sharing volume becomes the binding constraint — Personal Lite lists 50 GB for $57 a year and Personal Essential 1 TB for $143.88 a year. The business tiers are a different purchase, bought for administrative control rather than for space. Business is $228 per user per year, or $24 per user per month, with a floor of 3 users, so a solo operator or a pair funds a seat nobody occupies. Upgrading also does not remove the per-file ceiling, which still stops at 20 GB on the highest self-serve tier, and it does not unlock dynamic watermarking or AzureAD and Okta provisioning for a team below 5 users, because those features sit in Business Pro.

How to actually use Tresorit

The shortest route to real value is the 14-day trial, which Tresorit offers on every plan without feature limitations. Personal, Professional and Business run through the vendor's own checkout and provision straight away. Business Pro does not: it is a lead form, and Tresorit states that its team enables the trial and reaches out by email once the workspace is set up, so expect a wait rather than an instant login. Two details at the order screen matter. The card price is driven by a Monthly/Yearly toggle carrying a 20% OFF badge, so the headline figure changes depending on which side is selected. And seat minimums are enforced up front, 3 users on Business and 5 on Business Pro, so a smaller team has to overbuy. Plan changes afterwards live in the Admin Center/Account Portal, under Billing, then Modify plan, Plan type and Apply changes, ending on Upgrade. Cancelling in the same place downgrades the account to Basic instead of closing it. Frequently asked questions

Dropbox vs Tresorit — what actually differs?

Not the storage, the key. Tresorit is end-to-end encrypted and zero-knowledge, which means Tresorit itself cannot read your files — and neither can a server-side search, so you give up content search, real-time co-editing and, most consequentially, password recovery. Dropbox keeps all three because it can read your data. Buy Tresorit when a confidentiality obligation makes that trade worth it, and write a credential-recovery procedure before you migrate, because there is nobody who can reset it for you.

Why is the Tresorit price I see different from the one quoted elsewhere?

Because the storefront prices in your local currency. On 4 August 2026 we read Professional, Business and Business Pro at $33.99, $24 and $30 per user per month; on 14 August 2026 the same pages served us €29.99, €20 and €25. Neither reading is wrong — check which currency your own checkout is in before you compare against anyone else's quote.

Can I trial Tresorit without handing over card details?

No. The trial runs 14 days on any plan with no feature limits, but Tresorit's own FAQ states that payment information is required even during the trial period, and Business Pro is not self-serve at all — that one is a form, and their team enables the workspace and emails you. Seat minimums apply from the start too: 3 users on Business, 5 on Business Pro.

Is Tresorit secure and reliable enough to depend on?

Judge it on evidence rather than adjectives. Look for a published security page, single sign-on and role controls on the plan you would actually buy (they are often gated to higher tiers), a status page with real incident history, and a clear answer on where your data is stored. If the tool will hold anything sensitive, those four answers matter more than any feature comparison.

Who should not use Tresorit?

Skip it if you want cheap bulk storage or deep app integrations (mainstream clouds win), or a personal-grade E2EE like Proton Drive covers your needs at lower cost. Buying a tool to fix a problem you don't have yet just adds cost and another login to manage.
