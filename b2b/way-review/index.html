Four tiers, and above the first one the ladder is a location counter rather than a feature list. Free carries one store location, the floating Google Maps locator, appearance customisation and online support, with the app's own branding shown on your storefront. Basic is $6.99 a month or $67.10 a year and removes that branding, raising the limit to 50 locations and adding the map widget as an app block, a pop-up modal for multiple locations, full customisation with custom CSS, and bulk import and export.

Advanced at $14.99 a month or $143.90 a year and Pro at $29.99 or $287.90 list exactly the same eight features as Basic; what they add is headroom, 500 locations and 2,000. Annual billing saves a stated 20% on every paid tier and each carries a 7-day free trial. No Google Maps API key is required on any plan. Charges are in USD and recur every 30 days.

One line in the feature bullets sits against all of that: it advertises unlimited store locations, which no plan card offers. Developer Effective Apps, Ashdod, Israel; the app launched 24 April 2020, carries Shopify's Built for Shopify badge, names eleven interface languages and works with Google Maps, Apple Maps, Mapbox and Shopify's B2B solution. What the price did

We capture Way’s own pricing page every month and keep the figures, so this is our record of the page — not a forecast, and not someone else’s summary.

Every paid tier came down between our two readings, and by roughly the same three-tenths. On 25 August 2026 the listing priced Basic at $9.99 a month, Advanced at $19.99 and Pro at $39.99, with annual rates of $95.90, $191.90 and $383.90. On 16 September 2026 the same four cards read $6.99, $14.99 and $29.99, annually $67.10, $143.90 and $287.90. Nothing else moved: the same eight features on all three paid tiers, the same 1, 50, 500 and 2,000 location limits, the same 20% annual discount and the same 7-day trial. This is a price cut rather than a repackaging, so an older quote for this app is now too high by about a third.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Basic, Advanced and Pro list the same eight capabilities, so the $8 and $23 steps above Basic buy nothing but a higher location limit, and the jump from 50 to 500 leaves no tier in between. A chain with 60 or 80 shops pays the 500-location price and uses a tenth of it. The free plan is genuinely usable on a live store, which is worth saying plainly — the same developer's Blocky and TiXel listings advertise free plans that turn out to be development stores only, and this one is not that.

What it does carry is the app's branding on your storefront and a hard limit of one location, so it is a real free tier for a single shop rather than a trial in disguise.

When the paid plan is worth it

The three paid tiers carry word-for-word identical feature lists, so the only thing you are ever buying above $6.99 is room. That makes the arithmetic unusually simple and unusually easy to get wrong. Per location, Basic works out at fourteen cents a month, Advanced at three cents and Pro at one and a half — but only if you fill the tier. So count your locations before you read the plans.

At 60 shops you are paying $14.99 for a ceiling of 500, which is $14.99 for the ten locations that spilled over Basic. The steps are wide enough that most retailers sit a long way below their own ceiling, and there is no tier between 50 and 500 to land on. The first step is the one that is not about volume at all. Free is capped at one location, but what most single-shop merchants actually pay $6.99 for is the removal of the app's branding from their storefront.

Judge that step on whether you want another company's name under your map, not on the location count.

How to actually use Way

Count your locations first, because that number is the only thing the paid tiers differ on. Fifty is the Basic ceiling and the next stop is 500, so a chain in the sixties should know it is buying a ceiling it will not approach rather than a feature it needs. Then decide about branding separately. The free plan works on a live storefront with one location, which makes it a real place to start rather than a demo — but it puts the app's name under your map.

If that is the thing you want gone, $6.99 is the price of removing it, and the location limit is incidental to your decision. When you do load locations, use the bulk import rather than the form. It arrives at Basic along with custom CSS, and a spreadsheet of addresses is both faster and easier to correct later than fifty entries typed one at a time. Place the map as an app block rather than the floating widget if you want it on a specific page; both are on the same tier.

Frequently asked questions

The listing says unlimited locations — so why do the plans stop at 2,000?

Because the bullet and the plan cards are two different kinds of writing, and only one of them is the deal. Read on 19 September 2026, the feature list at the top of the listing promises you can add unlimited store locations. The pricing block underneath caps Free at one, Basic at 50, Advanced at 500 and Pro at 2,000, and those ceilings are the only thing separating the three paid tiers — their eight features are word for word identical. Treat the ceiling on the card you pay for as the number that counts and the bullet as marketing copy that has outrun the plans. If you genuinely have more than 2,000 stockists, do not install on the strength of that sentence: ask the developer in writing what happens past the Pro limit before you build a locator around it.

Do I need a Google Maps API key, and will Google bill me separately?

No key, and no second bill — this is the app's most quietly useful feature. Every tier, the free one included, states that no API key is required, which means you are not opening a Google Cloud project, not attaching a credit card to it, and not watching a map-loads meter that bills you when a campaign sends a rush of traffic at your store locator. That is the step that stops most merchants building this themselves, and it is also the difference between a fixed monthly cost and a usage bill you do not control. The app also lists Apple Maps, Mapbox and Shopify's B2B solution among the things it works with, so the Google dependency is not the whole story — but the key question, the one that decides whether a developer has to be involved at all, is settled on the free plan.

What happens when I pass my plan's location limit?

The listing does not say, and we looked. Each card names a ceiling — 1, 50, 500, 2,000 — and nothing on the page describes what the app does at that ceiling: no overage rate, no per-location charge, no note about the map refusing new entries. The only nearby wording is Shopify's standard line that recurring and usage-based charges are billed every 30 days, which is printed on every listing and says nothing specific here. It matters more on this app than on most, because bulk import from a spreadsheet arrives at Basic: it is genuinely easy to upload 60 addresses into a 50-location plan and find out afterwards which ten the app decided to drop. Ask support before the import, or upload in batches small enough to see the boundary as you cross it.

What should I weigh before buying E-commerce software?

Check your platform and version specifically. Shopify support is close to universal in this category, while WooCommerce, BigCommerce, Magento and headless setups vary a lot, and 'supported' sometimes means a generic API rather than a maintained app. A listing in your own platform's app store, with recent reviews there, is a better signal than the vendor's logo wall.
