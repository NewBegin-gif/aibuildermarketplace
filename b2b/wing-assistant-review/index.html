When the paid plan is worth it

There is no free tier here and there was never going to be — you are renting a person, so the first invoice is the entry price. The published choice is General Virtual Assistant part-time at $699 a month or full-time at $999, which Wing frames as a 16% saving against the part-time rate pro-rated. In cash terms the step up is $300 a month, or $3,600 a year, and it is worth taking only if you already have enough documented, delegatable work to fill the extra hours; an underused assistant is the most expensive outcome on this page.

The detail most likely to change your actual bill is not the plan. Wing's FAQ lists a $35 per month card processing fee that is waived for ACH and comparable bank transfers. On a part-time engagement that is $420 a year — a full 5% on top — for nothing but a payment method. Switching to bank transfer is the only lever on this page that costs nothing to pull, and it should be pulled before the first renewal.

Coverage is the second thing to settle before you commit. The FAQ documents default availability of 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, with the client choosing that window as a single unbroken block rather than splitting it across the day. For a team in one timezone that is fine; for a distributed team it can mean an assistant who is offline exactly when half the work lands, and that has to be raised on the 15-minute intake call, not after.

What the price does include is worth weighing against hiring directly: a dedicated assistant, a success manager, and free replacement if the fit is wrong. That replacement clause is the real argument for a managed service over a freelance marketplace — the cost of a bad match is absorbed rather than repeated. Two specialist tiers now carry a rate in Wing's llms.txt (28 September 2026): Executive Assistant at $1,299 and Super Assistant at $4,999 a month, both for 160 hours; other specialist roles and enterprise remain quoted, so they cannot be compared here.

How to actually use Wing Assistant

Wing has no self-serve signup; the shortest route to value runs through its booking flow. The vendor's how-it-works page describes the first step as a 15-minute call to identify which recurring work is landing on the wrong people; Wing then handles matching, setup and tool access, and says an assistant is up and running in under 48 hours. Everything after that lives in Wing Workspace, where the task queue, statuses and approval workflows sit.

Coverage and billing decide whether the first week pays off. Wing's FAQ documents default availability of 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, with the client choosing that window as a single unbroken block rather than splitting it, so distributed teams should raise coverage on that call. Billing is the other snag: the FAQ lists a $35 per month card processing fee, waived for ACH and comparable bank transfers.

Wing also asks for roughly 15 to 30 minutes a day of feedback in the first week, with most clients stepping back by week two; accounts that skip that calibration are where delegation stalls.

Frequently asked questions

What does Wing Assistant cost?

$699 a month for a part-time general virtual assistant (80 hours a month) and $999 for full-time (160 hours), read from their own pricing on 17 August and again on 28 September 2026. Wing's machine-readable site summary (llms.txt) now also lists two premium plans: an Executive Assistant at $1,299 a month and an Asia-based Super Assistant at $4,999, both 160 hours. Other specialist roles are quoted on a call, and paying by card adds $35 a month that bank transfer avoids.

Is full-time worth the extra $300?

On the arithmetic it usually is: $999 against $699 buys roughly double the hours, which is why Wing labels it a saving rather than an upgrade. The question is whether you have enough delegable work to fill the hours — an underused full-time assistant costs more per task than a busy part-time one.

What happens if the assistant is not a good fit?

Wing's published package includes free replacement along with a dedicated success manager, which is the practical difference from hiring directly. Treat the first month as the real trial and be specific with the success manager early rather than waiting.

What should I weigh before buying Operations & Workflow software?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.
