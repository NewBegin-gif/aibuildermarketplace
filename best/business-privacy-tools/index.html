The best privacy and encryption tools for business (2026)

Only after shared passwords? read our honest Passpack review → — what zero-knowledge encryption costs when you strip out the polish.
Short answer: The best business privacy tools are Proton for teams for whom encryption is a requirement rather than a preference — client material, legal or medical files — and who want mail, calendar, drive, VPN and a password manager under one account; Proton Drive for privacy-conscious businesses that want genuinely end-to-end-encrypted storage from a Swiss provider, ideally alongside the rest of the Proton suite; 1Password for teams that want the most polished, secure password manager with solid admin controls and integrations, and will pay for that polish. Below, what each is best for — and who should skip it.

Encryption is not one product, and the gap between two tools doing the same job is startling once you read the pricing pages. Team password managers are the clearest case: Passpack charges $20 per user per year for teams up to twenty (25 August 2026), while 1Password's Business tier is about $8.99 per user a month with no free tier at all (11 August 2026). That is roughly five times the money for the same core job — shared vaults, zero-knowledge encryption, audit trails — bought at a different level of polish and support. Storage splits the same way, except the split is about what the product even is. Proton Drive is $3.99 a month for 200 GB, billed $47.88 a year (26 August 2026). Tresorit's business plans run €16 to €23.99 per user a month on yearly billing (14 August 2026). IDrive is not really in that race: it is backup rather than sync, at $9.99 a month for 5 TB and shown at $6.99 on annual billing (27 August 2026). Work out which of the three jobs you are buying — a vault, a drive, or a backup — before you compare a single price, because the cheapest tool in the wrong category is not a saving.

How to choose

Split the problem into three before you shop. Credentials belong in a vault, files at rest in encrypted storage, and the network path is a VPN. One does not cover another, and most overspending here comes from buying a suite to solve one of the three. Then decide whether encryption is a requirement or a preference. If a provider being able to read your files is itself the problem — client material, legal or medical records — end-to-end encryption is non-negotiable and you accept what comes with it, starting with weaker search. If it is a preference, mainstream clouds give you more space and better collaboration for the money. Finally, price per user per year rather than per month, and look at what happens at renewal: the first year is where several of these look cheapest and the second year is where they are actually bought.

Frequently asked questions

What does end-to-end encrypted actually mean here?

That the provider cannot read your files, because the keys never leave your devices. It is a real guarantee with a real cost: Proton Drive has no server-side full-text search precisely because Proton cannot see inside your documents. If searching your whole drive matters more than the provider being unable to read it, a mainstream cloud is the honest answer.

Why is Passpack so much cheaper than 1Password?

Because you are buying the same cryptography with less product around it. Passpack's Teams plan is $20 per user per year for up to twenty people, read on 25 August 2026; 1Password Business is about $8.99 per user a month, re-verified 11 August 2026. The gap is interface, mobile apps, integrations and support — not the strength of the vault.

Is a VPN part of a privacy stack or a separate thing?

Separate. A VPN protects the network path; a vault protects credentials; encrypted storage protects files at rest. They solve different problems and buying one does not cover the others. NordVPN is here because remote teams keep asking, not because it substitutes for the rest.
