What an aspect ratio actually is

An aspect ratio is simply the relationship between a thing's width and its height, written as two numbers like 16:9 or 1:1. It says nothing about size — a 16:9 image can be tiny or enormous — only about shape. That distinction matters because the shape is what has to fit the frame it lands in, whether that's a YouTube player, a phone screen held upright, or a square post in a feed. This calculator works out the missing dimension when you know the ratio and one side, so you can resize confidently without distorting anything. Understanding ratios is the difference between content that fills its frame cleanly and content that arrives stretched, squashed or awkwardly cropped.

Why it matters more than people think

Get the ratio wrong and one of two ugly things happens. Either the platform crops your content to fit — silently chopping off the top of a head or the edge of your text — or it letterboxes it, pillowing your image with black bars that shrink it and make it look amateur. Neither is what you designed. Because every platform expects a particular shape, publishing the same file everywhere guarantees it's wrong somewhere. Matching the ratio to the destination isn't fussiness; it's the baseline for looking professional, and it's one of the most common things that separates content that feels native to a platform from content that feels clumsily reposted.

The ratios worth knowing

A handful of ratios cover almost everything. 16:9 is the widescreen standard for horizontal video and most screens. 9:16 is its vertical mirror — the full-screen phone shape used by short-form video and stories. 1:1 is the square, long a feed favourite. 4:5 is the slightly taller portrait that takes up more vertical space in a mobile feed without going full-screen. And 21:9 is the ultra-wide cinematic look. Knowing these by name, and roughly what each is for, lets you plan content around its final home instead of discovering the mismatch after you've shot or designed it.

Match the ratio to the platform

Each platform has strong preferences, and fighting them costs you reach. Horizontal video platforms want 16:9. Short-form vertical video and full-screen stories want 9:16. Feed images often perform best as square or 4:5 portrait, because they claim more of the scrolling screen. Getting this right isn't just aesthetic — platforms often favour content that fits their native format, and viewers subconsciously trust content that looks made-for-here over content that looks recycled from somewhere else. Before you create, decide where it's going, and let the destination dictate the shape rather than trying to force one shape to work everywhere.

Resizing without wrecking it

The maths of resizing while keeping a ratio is straightforward: if you change the width, the height has to change by the same proportion, and vice versa. That's exactly what this calculator does — you lock the ratio, change one dimension, and it gives you the other. The cardinal sin is changing width and height independently, which stretches or squashes the image and makes faces look wrong in a way viewers notice even if they can't name it. Always scale proportionally. When you genuinely need a different shape, you crop or add space deliberately — you never just drag one edge and distort the whole thing.

Cropping versus stretching

When content must move between ratios, you have two honest options and one dishonest one. You can crop — cut off part of the image to fit the new shape, losing content at the edges but keeping everything undistorted. You can add space — letterbox or pad the image so it fits without cropping, keeping everything but shrinking the subject. What you must never do is stretch, forcing the wrong shape to fill the frame, because that warps every proportion in the image. Cropping loses information; padding wastes space; stretching destroys the look entirely. Between the two acceptable choices, cropping usually looks more professional as long as you protect the important part of the frame.

Shoot for multiple ratios from the start

If you know a video or photo will be published in several places, plan for it while you create rather than crop in a panic later. The technique is to keep the important action within a "safe zone" — a central region that survives cropping to both the widest and the tallest ratio you'll need. Frame a little loose, keep faces and key text away from the edges, and a single capture can be cropped to 16:9, 1:1 and 9:16 without losing anything essential. Creators who shoot with the final ratios in mind get several native-looking outputs from one shoot; those who don't end up either recropping badly or reshooting.

Ratio is not resolution

A common confusion worth clearing up: aspect ratio and resolution are different things. Ratio is the shape; resolution is how many pixels fill it. Two videos can both be 16:9 while one is crisp 4K and the other a blurry low-res file. You need the right ratio so the content fits the frame, and enough resolution so it looks sharp at the size it's displayed. Getting the ratio right but the resolution too low gives you a correctly-shaped but fuzzy result; plenty of pixels in the wrong ratio still gets cropped or letterboxed. Both matter, and they're set independently — so check both before you export.

Frequently asked questions

How do I keep an image's aspect ratio?

Scale width and height by the same factor. Enter your original dimensions and the new width here, and it calculates the height that preserves the ratio.

What aspect ratio should I use for video?

16:9 for YouTube and landscape video, 9:16 for vertical (Reels, TikTok, Shorts), 1:1 for square posts. Match the platform you're publishing to.

What does a ratio like 16:9 mean?
