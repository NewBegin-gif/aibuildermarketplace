Your signature is the most-seen thing you never designed

Think about how many emails you send in a year — every single one ends with your signature. It's quietly one of the most-viewed pieces of communication your business produces, seen by clients, prospects, partners and suppliers, over and over. Yet most people either leave it as a bare name, or worse, a broken jumble of mismatched fonts and stretched logos assembled once in five minutes and never touched again.

A clean signature won't close deals on its own, but it signals the same thing a firm handshake does: this person has their act together. This generator builds one properly, so the thing attached to thousands of your messages works for you instead of against you.

What belongs in a signature — and what doesn't

A good signature answers three questions fast: who are you, what do you do, and how do I reach you. That means your name, role and company; one or two contact methods; and optionally your website and one or two social profiles that actually matter professionally. What doesn't belong: your entire contact card, six social icons, an inspirational quote, a wall of legal disclaimers nobody reads, and giant banner images.

Every extra element dilutes the ones that matter. The test is ruthless — if a line doesn't help the recipient identify you or take a next step, it's decoration, and decoration in email mostly reads as clutter.

Why HTML signatures break — and how to build one that doesn't

Email is the most hostile design environment in software. The same signature renders differently in Outlook, Gmail, Apple Mail and every phone client, because each supports a different, ancient subset of HTML and CSS. Modern layout techniques simply don't work; what survives everywhere is old-fashioned table-based structure, inline styles, and web-safe fonts. This is why hand-crafted signatures pasted from a design tool so often arrive mangled — the design was built for a browser, not for Outlook's rendering engine.

A generator that outputs conservative, table-based HTML with inline styles isn't being old-fashioned; it's being realistic about where the signature has to live.

The image trap

Images in signatures cause more problems than any other element. Many clients block images by default, so a signature that's one big image renders as an empty box with a sad broken-image icon. Attach the image instead of hosting it, and every reply chain fills with 'signature.png' attachments. Host it, and you need a reliable, permanent URL — move or delete the file and every past email breaks retroactively. The safe pattern: keep essential information as real text (never baked into an image), use at most one small hosted image (a logo or headshot), always set alt text, and define explicit width and height so blocked images don't collapse the layout. Text-first signatures survive everything; image-first signatures are a gamble on every recipient's settings.

Keep it consistent across the team

For a company, the signature is a small but real branding surface — and nothing looks sloppier than five employees with five different formats, fonts and levels of effort. Agreeing one template, with fixed order and styling and per-person details slotted in, costs nothing and instantly makes every outgoing email look like it came from the same organisation. It also spares each new hire from improvising their own. If you're the founder, make the template once, document it, and hand it to everyone; the generator above makes producing each person's variant a two-minute job rather than a design exercise.

Legal lines and the disclaimer question

Depending on where you operate, some information may genuinely belong in your signature — many jurisdictions expect registered business details on commercial correspondence, and regulated industries add their own requirements. Check what applies to you rather than copying someone else's block. What's rarely required is the sprawling multi-paragraph confidentiality disclaimer that pads so many corporate signatures; its legal force is widely questioned, and its practical effect is mostly to make every email longer.

If you need legal lines, keep them small, grey and at the very bottom — present for compliance, invisible to the eye.

Mobile is half your audience

A large share of email is read on phones, where a wide multi-column signature turns into a squashed mess and tiny text becomes unreadable. Keep the signature narrow — comfortably under 400 pixels — so it survives a phone screen without horizontal scrolling. Short lines, adequate font sizes, and a single-column structure when in doubt. And test it: send yourself the signature and open it on your own phone, in at least Gmail and Apple Mail, before rolling it out.

Two minutes of testing catches the layout breaks that would otherwise ride along on every message you send for the next year.

Treat it as a tiny, permanent campaign

Because the signature travels with every email, it's also modest marketing real estate. A single, quietly-styled line — linking to your latest guide, product launch or booking page — rides along on all your correspondence at zero cost. The discipline is restraint: one link, updated when it goes stale, never a banner circus. And staleness is the real enemy: the signature that still lists your old job title, dead phone number or expired promotion actively spreads wrong information with every send.

Put a reminder in your calendar to glance at it quarterly; it's the cheapest audit you'll ever do.
