Why good emails end up in spam

You can write a perfectly honest, useful email and still watch it vanish into spam folders — because whether an email lands in the inbox has surprisingly little to do with whether it's actually spam. Filters make a probabilistic judgement from dozens of signals, and a legitimate message that trips enough of them gets buried. This checker flags the content signals that push you toward the spam folder, but the fuller picture matters: deliverability is a system, and understanding it is the difference between an email that reaches people and one that quietly disappears despite being exactly what your subscribers asked for.

The two halves of deliverability

Getting to the inbox rests on two distinct pillars, and neglecting either sinks you. The first is content — the words, links, formatting and structure of the individual email, which is what this checker examines. The second is infrastructure and reputation — whether your domain is properly authenticated, whether your sending server is trusted, and whether recipients have historically engaged with your mail. A beautifully-worded email from an untrusted, unauthenticated sender still lands in spam; a plain one from a trusted sender sails through.

You have to get both halves right, and most deliverability problems that feel mysterious are really the infrastructure half being quietly broken.

The content signals filters watch

On the content side, filters react to a familiar set of red flags. Classic spam-trigger words and phrases — the "free money," "act now," "100% guaranteed" vocabulary — raise suspicion. So do ALL-CAPS subject lines, walls of exclamation marks, lots of dollar signs, and huge risky-looking fonts or colours. An email that's one big image with almost no text looks evasive to filters. Too many links, or links to dodgy domains, hurt.

And a mismatch between a spammy subject and the body reads as bait. None of these individually condemns you, but they accumulate — which is exactly why running your draft through a checker to catch the avoidable ones is worth the minute it takes.

The technical foundation: SPF, DKIM, DMARC

The infrastructure half starts with three authentication standards that prove you are who you say you are. SPF declares which servers are allowed to send mail for your domain. DKIM cryptographically signs your messages so they can't be tampered with in transit. DMARC ties the two together and tells receiving servers what to do with mail that fails. Without these set up correctly, major mailbox providers increasingly treat your mail as suspicious by default — and recent tightening of sender requirements has made proper authentication effectively mandatory rather than optional.

If your emails are mysteriously landing in spam, misconfigured authentication is one of the first things to check.

Sender reputation is everything

Behind the scenes, your sending domain and IP carry a reputation score that mailbox providers build from your history — and it may be the single biggest factor in whether you reach the inbox. Send to engaged recipients who open and reply, keep complaints and bounces low, and your reputation rises and your mail flows. Send to stale lists, generate spam complaints, or hit spam traps, and it craters — after which even perfect content lands in spam.

Reputation is earned slowly and lost quickly, which is why deliverability is a long game of consistently good sending behaviour, not a trick you apply to a single email.

Engagement tells the real story

Modern filters lean heavily on how recipients actually behave with your mail. Opens, clicks and replies are positive signals; being deleted unread, ignored, or marked as spam are powerful negative ones. A single spam complaint carries far more weight than most senders realise. This is why sending to people who genuinely want your email matters so much more than sending to as many people as possible — an engaged small list outperforms a large indifferent one, because the engaged list teaches filters that your mail is wanted.

Chasing list size at the expense of engagement is one of the fastest ways to destroy your own deliverability.

List hygiene

Following from that, keeping your list clean is deliverability maintenance you can't skip. Old, unengaged addresses drag down your engagement metrics and may include spam traps — addresses specifically used to catch senders mailing stale lists. Regularly removing subscribers who never open, verifying addresses before you add them, and honouring unsubscribes promptly all protect your reputation. It feels counterintuitive to shrink your own list, but pruning dead weight lifts your engagement rate and your inbox placement for everyone who remains.

A smaller, cleaner, engaged list reliably beats a big, dusty one that's quietly poisoning your sender score.

Test before you send

Because so much can go wrong, testing before a big send is basic hygiene. Run the content through a checker for trigger words and structural issues, send test messages to accounts on the major providers to see where they land, and confirm your authentication is passing. Catching a spam-flavoured subject line or a broken DKIM signature before you hit send to your whole list is far cheaper than discovering it afterwards from your open rates.

A few minutes of pre-flight checks routinely saves a campaign from the spam folder — and gives you a chance to fix the avoidable problems while it still matters. It's also worth checking how your email renders with images blocked, since many recipients see that version first, and confirming your unsubscribe link works — a broken one drives spam complaints faster than almost anything else.

Frequently asked questions

Do spam-trigger words really matter?

They contribute to your spam score. Words like 'free', 'act now' and '$$$', plus heavy caps and exclamation marks, raise the risk — but they're one factor among many.

What else affects deliverability?

Domain authentication (SPF/DKIM/DMARC), sender reputation, list quality and engagement. A DMARC monitor like EasyDMARC shows whether the authentication half is actually passing, and who else is sending as your domain.

Does this scan my actual deliverability?

No — it checks your wording locally. Real inbox placement depends on your sending setup, which an authenticated provider manages.
