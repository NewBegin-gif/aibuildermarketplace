The subject line decides the open — nothing else gets a vote

Before a single word of your carefully-written email is read, the subject line has already done its job or failed at it. In a crowded inbox, a recipient gives each subject a fraction of a second: open, ignore, or delete. That makes those few words the highest-leverage text in the entire email — a great campaign with a weak subject line performs like a weak campaign, because almost nobody sees the difference.

This tester scores the mechanical factors that reliably help or hurt: length, spam triggers, formatting habits. The craft on top of that is yours, and the sections below cover both.

Length: write for the phone, not the desktop

Most email is opened on phones, and phones truncate subject lines aggressively — often around 30 to 40 characters, well short of what a desktop shows. That's why the practical sweet spot sits around 30 to 50 characters: long enough to say something real, short enough to survive a mobile inbox intact. More important than the exact count is front-loading: put the words that carry the message first, so even a clipped subject still lands.

'Your invoice is overdue — action needed' survives truncation; 'Action needed regarding the status of your…' dies at exactly the wrong moment.

The spam triggers that sink deliverability

Certain habits read as spam to both filters and humans: ALL-CAPS words, stacked exclamation marks, dollar signs, and the classic vocabulary — free, guarantee, act now, limited time, winner. One of these won't doom you; pile up several and you're signalling exactly like the mail people mark as junk, which trains filters to bury you. The deeper point is that these tricks don't even work on humans anymore: urgency theatrics have been so abused that they lower trust rather than raise it.

The tester flags them so you can say the same thing in plainer, more credible words — which almost always performs better anyway.

Curiosity, clarity and the credibility line

The eternal subject-line tension: clarity tells the reader exactly what's inside; curiosity leaves a gap they want to close. Both work, and the best lines often blend them — concrete enough to feel real, open enough to invite the click. What never works long-term is the bait-and-switch: a subject that promises something the email doesn't deliver wins one open and loses a subscriber's trust permanently. Your subject line is a promise, and your list learns very quickly whether your promises mean anything.

Write every subject as if the reader will judge your next ten emails by whether this one told the truth — because they will.

Personalisation and emoji: useful, in small doses

A first name in the subject can lift opens — until everyone does it mechanically and it reads as mail-merge. Real personalisation is relevance: referencing the thing the recipient actually did, bought or cares about beats inserting their name into a generic line. Emoji are similar: one well-chosen emoji can add warmth or catch the eye in a monochrome inbox; three read as spam, and some clients render them inconsistently.

Both tools follow the same rule — they amplify a good subject line, they don't rescue a bad one, and overuse burns the effect for everyone.

The preview text you're probably wasting

Next to the subject, inboxes show a snippet of preview text — and most senders leave it to chance, letting 'View this email in your browser' or raw header junk fill the slot. That's free real estate for a second line of persuasion: a good preheader extends the subject's promise, adds a detail, or answers the obvious objection. Think of subject and preview as a headline and subheadline working together.

Setting the preheader deliberately is one of the easiest wins in email marketing, precisely because so few senders bother.

Test with data, not opinions

Nobody — not you, not this tool, not a copywriting guru — reliably knows which of two decent subject lines will win with your specific audience. That's what A/B testing is for: send variant A to part of your list, variant B to another, and let the open rate decide. Do it consistently and patterns emerge that are true for your audience — which tones, lengths and hooks they respond to — knowledge worth far more than any generic best practice.

One caveat: since mail-privacy features inflate open rates, watch clicks and downstream action too, not opens alone. The direction is what matters: test, keep the winners' lessons, repeat.

Learn from your own inbox

The cheapest subject-line education available is the inbox you already own. Scroll your promotions tab and notice, honestly, which subjects you opened this week and which you deleted unread — then ask why. Usually the openers were specific, timely and from senders whose past emails delivered value, while the deleted pile is a wall of interchangeable hype. Do the same with your own send history: your last twenty subjects, ranked by open rate, are a private masterclass in what your audience responds to.

Patterns that survive this audit — a certain directness, a certain length, questions versus statements — are worth more than any generic swipe file, because they are proven on the only audience that matters: yours.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an email subject line be?

Around 30–50 characters is the sweet spot — long enough to be clear, short enough not to get cut off on mobile inboxes, which often truncate near 50 characters.

Do spam-trigger words really matter?

They contribute. Modern spam filters look at many signals, but words like 'free', 'act now' and '$$$', combined with lots of caps and exclamation marks, raise your risk of landing in spam.

Should I use emoji in subject lines?

One relevant emoji can boost opens and stand out in a crowded inbox. Several look spammy and can trigger filters. Test it with your own audience.
