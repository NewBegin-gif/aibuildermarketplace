The headline does most of the work

David Ogilvy's old line still holds: on average, far more people read the headline than ever read the body beneath it. On a crowded feed or a search results page, the headline is the ad for the article — if it fails, nothing else you wrote gets a chance. That's why sharpening a headline is one of the highest-leverage minutes you can spend on any piece of content. The words underneath might be brilliant, but they're invisible until the headline earns the click.

This tool scores the parts of a headline that tend to move that decision, so you can improve it before you publish rather than wondering later why great content went unread.

The jobs a headline has to do at once

A strong headline juggles several jobs in a handful of words. It has to signal relevance — this is about the thing you care about — so the reader knows they're in the right place. It has to promise value — here's what you'll get out of reading. It has to spark enough curiosity that stopping feels better than scrolling past. And it has to set an honest expectation the article can actually meet.

Get relevance and value without curiosity and it reads flat; get curiosity without honesty and you've written clickbait that burns trust. The best headlines hold all of these in tension, which is why writing them is genuinely hard and worth a second opinion.

Length and where headlines get read

Length matters because context clips it. In search results a headline gets truncated past roughly sixty characters; in an email subject line the mobile cut-off is even tighter; on social the first few words carry the weight before the rest scrolls out of view. As a rough guide, headlines around six to twelve words tend to be scannable without being cut off. But the deeper rule is front-loading: put the most important, most intriguing words first, so the headline still lands even when it's clipped. Assume the end will be lost and make the beginning do the selling.

Numbers, specificity and power words

Three levers reliably lift a headline. Numbers add concreteness and a promise of structure — "7 ways" tells the reader exactly what they're getting and how much of it. Specificity beats vagueness every time: "cut your AWS bill 30% in a weekend" outperforms "save money on hosting" because it's vivid and testable. And certain emotional or "power" words — honest, proven, mistake, avoid, effortless — add a charge that neutral phrasing lacks.

The caution is balance: a headline that stacks every trick at once ("The 7 Shocking, Proven Secrets Experts Don't Want You To Know") reads as spam. Reach for one or two levers, not all of them.

Emotion versus rational appeal

Headlines pull on two ropes: emotion and logic. Emotional headlines tap curiosity, fear of missing out, surprise or aspiration, and they travel well on social feeds where people react fast. Rational headlines promise a clear, useful outcome and tend to perform where the reader is already problem-aware — search results, documentation, comparison pages. Neither is universally better; the right blend depends on where the headline will live and how ready the reader is to act.

A useful habit is to draft one emotional version and one rational version of the same headline, then judge which fits the channel and the intent you're writing for.

Curiosity without clickbait

There's a bright line between curiosity and clickbait, and it's honesty. Curiosity opens a genuine gap — a surprising angle, a counterintuitive claim, a specific promise — that the article then actually closes. Clickbait opens a gap it never fills, or wildly overstates what's inside. The short-term click is the same; the long-term result is not. Readers remember the source that wasted their time and stop trusting it, while the outlet that consistently delivers on its headlines builds an audience that clicks on the strength of the name alone.

For any brand that plans to be around next year, the honest headline is not just the ethical choice — it's the profitable one.

You still have to test

Every guideline here is a starting point, not a verdict, because your specific audience gets the only real vote. Two headlines that both look strong on paper can perform very differently in the wild, and the gap is often large enough to matter. That's why serious publishers test: they run two or three headline variants and let the click-through rate decide, rather than trusting the loudest opinion in the room.

Even a rough test beats a confident guess. Treat the score this tool gives you as a way to generate better candidates to test — not as a substitute for finding out what your readers actually respond to.

Subject lines and social hooks are headlines too

Everything here applies well beyond article titles, because a headline is really any first line that has to earn the next click. An email subject line is a headline with an even harsher cut-off and an even more distracted reader — the same rules about front-loading, specificity and honesty decide whether it's opened or ignored. The opening line of a social post is a headline competing against an entire scrolling feed.

A YouTube title, a landing-page hero, a cold-outreach first sentence: all of them live or die on the same handful of levers. Learning to write and judge headlines is therefore one of the highest-transfer skills in all of marketing, because you practise it dozens of times a week across every channel. Score your subject lines and social hooks with the same discipline you'd give a blog title, and the compounding returns show up everywhere you publish.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a good headline?

A number, 6–12 words, a power or emotional word, and a clear benefit — kept under about 60 characters. This tool scores those signals.

How long should a headline be?

Around 6–12 words and under ~60 characters works well for both readers and search results, where longer titles get cut off.

Does a high score guarantee clicks?

No — it means the structure is strong, but the headline still has to promise something readers want. Pair it with content that delivers, which a tool like Frase helps you write.
