How NPS works

Net Promoter Score comes from one question — how likely are you to recommend us, on a 0 to 10 scale — and sorts answers into three groups: promoters (9–10), passives (7–8) and detractors (0–6). Your score is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors, landing somewhere from -100 to +100. Above 0 is decent, above 50 is excellent — but the number alone is only half the point.

The trend beats the number

An absolute NPS is nearly meaningless out of context: scores vary hugely by industry, culture and how you phrase the ask. What matters is your own trend over time. A score climbing from 20 to 35 tells you far more than a rival's 40, because you can act on your own movement. Chasing someone else's benchmark is a distraction; beating last quarter is the goal.

The gold is in the follow-up

The score is a headline; the value is in the open-ended why behind it. Detractors tell you exactly what to fix, and reaching out can turn some around; promoters tell you what to double down on and are your warmest source of referrals and testimonials. A team that reads the comments and closes the loop gets far more from NPS than one that just reports the figure.

Where NPS misleads

Treat it as a directional signal, not gospel. It's easy to game (survey only happy customers, or right after a win), the sample skews toward people with strong feelings, and one number can hide an audience that half-loves and half-hates you. Use it to spot movement and surface reasons — not as the single metric that runs the business.

Reading the three groups honestly

NPS's categories carry real information beyond the headline score. Promoters (9-10) are your referral engine and expansion revenue — worth actively activating with review requests and referral programs, not just counting. Passives (7-8) are satisfied-but-unmoved: they don't hurt the score but silently defect to a better offer, and they're often one fixed annoyance away from becoming promoters — the cheapest score improvement available. Detractors (0-6) are both risk (churn, bad word of mouth) and gift: they're telling you, before leaving, what's broken. A team that only watches the aggregate score wastes three-quarters of what the survey actually collected.

The comment is worth more than the number

The follow-up question — "what's the main reason for your score?" — is where NPS earns its keep. Scores tell you *that* something changed; comments tell you *what*. Read them raw and unfiltered monthly, tag them into a handful of themes (pricing, support speed, missing feature X), and watch which themes grow. One pattern to respect: when multiple detractors independently name the same specific thing, it's not anecdote anymore. The companies that get value from NPS treat it as a structured feedback pipeline with a number attached; the ones that don't treat it as a number with annoying homework attached.

Survey mechanics decide your score before customers do

Two surveys of the same customers can produce scores twenty points apart on mechanics alone. Timing: surveying right after successful onboarding inflates; after a support ticket, deflates; the honest approach is a steady rhythm (say, quarterly per customer) independent of mood-moments. Channel: in-app responses skew toward engaged users; email reaches the quiet middle. Response rate is the silent killer — at 8% response, you're hearing the delighted and the furious, and the score mostly measures who bothered. Chase a response rate above 20-30% before treating movements as real, and never change mechanics mid-stream if you want your trend to mean anything.

Relationship NPS versus transactional NPS

One acronym, two instruments. Relationship NPS — the periodic "how likely are you to recommend us overall" — measures the health of the whole bond and moves slowly. Transactional NPS fires after a specific event (support ticket closed, order delivered) and measures that touchpoint. Mixing them into one trend line creates noise that looks like signal: a support-heavy month drags the blend down regardless of overall sentiment. Run them separately with separate baselines. For most SMBs, quarterly relationship NPS plus transactional pulses on the one or two touchpoints that matter is plenty — more surveying than that fatigues the very customers you're measuring.

Close the loop or don't bother surveying

The highest-leverage NPS practice has nothing to do with measurement: it's responding. A detractor who gets a personal, non-defensive reply within a day — "you're right, here's what we're doing about it" — converts to a promoter at a rate that feels unreasonable, because being heard is rarer than being satisfied. Promoters deserve a loop too: a thank-you plus a well-timed review or referral ask harvests the goodwill the score merely records. Teams that survey without closing the loop are training customers that feedback disappears into a void — which suppresses future response rates and quietly makes every subsequent score less trustworthy. The survey is the sensor; the follow-up is the product. Assign every detractor response an owner and a deadline, review the loop weekly, and the score starts moving for reasons you can actually name.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good NPS score?

Anything above 0 means more promoters than detractors. Above 30 is great and above 50 is excellent, though benchmarks vary by industry.

How is NPS calculated?

Percentage of promoters (9–10) minus percentage of detractors (0–6). Passives (7–8) count toward the total but not the score. This tool does the maths.

How do I collect NPS responses?

Send the 0–10 recommendation question to customers with a survey tool like SurveyMonkey, then drop the counts in here.
