QR codes earned their comeback

Declared dead a decade ago, QR codes came roaring back when phone cameras started scanning them natively — no app, just point and tap. Suddenly the friction vanished, and with it every excuse not to use them: menus, payments, event check-ins, product packaging, business cards, posters. A QR code is simply a bridge from the physical world to a URL, and this generator builds that bridge in seconds, client-side, with no signup and no expiry games.

The code you make here is yours forever. What's worth understanding is how the technology actually behaves — because a few practical rules separate codes that scan instantly from codes that frustrate everyone.

How a QR code actually works

A QR code encodes your text or URL as a grid of black and white modules, read by a camera in any orientation thanks to the three big corner squares that anchor its position. The more characters you encode, the denser the grid becomes — which is why a short URL produces a clean, chunky code and a long tracking-parameter monster produces a fine-grained one that's harder to scan at a distance or small size.

Practical rule one, then: encode the shortest URL you can. Density isn't aesthetic; it directly determines how forgiving your code is of small print sizes, cheap cameras and imperfect lighting.

Error correction: the hidden superpower

QR codes carry built-in redundancy — error correction that lets a scanner reconstruct the data even when part of the code is damaged, dirty or covered. Depending on the level, up to roughly a third of the code can be unreadable and it still scans. This is why codes survive scuffed packaging and why designers can place a small logo over the centre without breaking anything. The trade-off: higher error correction means a denser grid for the same content.

For most uses the default is right; go higher when the code will live somewhere rough — outdoor posters, packaging, anything handled — and resist logo overlays bigger than the redundancy can absorb.

Static versus dynamic — the choice that matters most

A static code, like this tool makes, encodes your destination directly: free, permanent, no middleman, works forever. Its one limitation is that the destination is baked in — reprint if you need to change it. A dynamic code points to a short redirect URL you control, so you can change the destination later and see scan analytics (when, where, what device) — but it depends on the redirect service staying alive and often costs a subscription.

The honest rule: for anything with a stable destination — a menu, a business card, a manual — static is strictly better, because nothing can rot. For campaigns you'll measure, update or reroute, dynamic earns its keep. Know which job you're doing before you print.

Size, contrast and placement — where codes fail in the wild

Most scanning failures are physical, not digital. Print the code too small for its distance and cameras can't resolve it — the rough guide is a scanning distance to size ratio of about ten to one, so a code scanned from ten centimetres can be one centimetre wide, but a poster read from across a room needs to be much larger. Keep strong contrast: dark modules on a light background, never the reverse gimmick that some scanners choke on, and never on a busy photo background.

Preserve the quiet zone — the empty margin around the code — because crowding it with text or borders genuinely breaks scans. And put it somewhere reachable: a QR code on a highway billboard is a joke that keeps getting reprinted.

Always test before you print

The five-second habit that prevents the expensive mistake: scan your own code, from a real phone, at the real size and distance, before anything goes to the printer. Test the destination too — on mobile, because that's where every scanner lands. A URL that works on desktop but redirects badly on phones, or a page that isn't mobile-friendly, wastes the scan you worked to earn. If the stakes are high (packaging runs, signage), test with both an iPhone and an Android, in mediocre light. Every printed QR failure you've ever seen was skippable with this one habit.

Give people a reason to scan

A naked QR code is a mystery box, and people have learned to ignore mystery boxes. The codes that get scanned sit next to a short, specific promise: 'Scan for the menu', 'Scan to pay', 'Scan for the setup video'. Tell people what they'll get and the scan rate multiplies; leave it implicit and only the curious bother. This also has a security angle — as QR-based phishing has grown, people are rightly warier of anonymous codes in public places.

A clear label, a trusted context and a sensible destination URL are what make your code feel safe to scan, because scanning is ultimately an act of trust.

Where QR codes quietly shine

Beyond the obvious menu-and-poster uses, QR codes solve small business problems elegantly. On packaging, they carry instructions, warranty registration or reorder links without printing costs rising. On invoices, they can open a pre-filled payment page and measurably speed up getting paid. On business cards, a code linking to your contact file saves the retyping that loses leads. At events, they replace clipboards for check-in and feedback forms. On equipment, a code linking to the manual or maintenance log saves every future technician a search.

The pattern is always the same — anywhere a human would otherwise type a URL from paper, a QR code deletes that friction, and deleted friction is conversions kept.

Do these QR codes expire?

No. A static QR code (what this tool creates) has the link baked in, so it works forever. It just can't be edited after you print it — for that you need a dynamic QR code.

PNG or SVG — which should I download?
