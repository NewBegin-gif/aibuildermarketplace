Why converting between salary and hourly is trickier than it looks

On the surface, turning a salary into an hourly rate is a simple division, and turning an hourly rate into a salary is the reverse. But the naive maths hides a lot, and using it to compare a salaried job against contract work, or to price your own time, leads people badly astray. This calculator does the conversion instantly, but the real value is understanding what the raw number leaves out — because whether you're weighing a job offer, quoting as a contractor, or budgeting as an employer, the gap between the simple figure and the true one is where the important decisions live.

The naive divide and the standard year

The conventional shortcut assumes a full-time year of about 2,080 hours — forty hours a week across fifty-two weeks — and divides the annual salary by that. It's a fine starting point and it's how most quick conversions are done. But that 2,080 figure quietly assumes you work every single week with no time off, which no salaried employee actually does. So while the standard divide gives a clean, comparable number, treat it as a reference point rather than the literal value of an hour, because the hours you're really paid for and the hours you actually work aren't the same.

What the simple number leaves out for employees

A salary comes wrapped in things an hourly figure ignores. Paid holiday and sick days mean a salaried worker is paid for weeks they don't work, which quietly raises their true hourly value above the naive divide. On top of the salary sit benefits — health cover, a pension contribution, paid leave — that can be worth a substantial fraction of the headline pay. So when a salaried role and an hourly rate look equal on paper, the salaried one is usually worth meaningfully more once its paid time off and benefits are counted. Comparing a bare hourly rate to a salary without adjusting for this is comparing an apple to a more expensive orange.

Gross versus take-home

Every conversion here works in gross terms — pay before tax — because tax depends on your specific situation, allowances and location. What actually reaches your bank account, the take-home, is lower, and the gap varies with your income and where you live. This matters when you're budgeting your real life around a number: the hourly rate that looks comfortable in gross can feel very different after tax and deductions. Use the gross conversion to compare offers on a like-for-like basis, but always run your own after-tax numbers before you make a life decision based on what an hour or a year "really" pays.

For employers: the true cost of an employee

If you're on the hiring side, an employee's salary is only part of what they cost you. On top of the salary come employer payroll taxes, benefits, insurance, equipment, software and overhead — which together commonly push the real cost to something like 1.25 to 1.4 times the salary, as a widely-used rule of thumb. So a worker on a given salary costs your business considerably more than that salary alone. Understanding this fully-loaded cost is essential for budgeting headcount honestly and for comparing an employee against a contractor, because the contractor's higher headline rate often looks very different once the employee's hidden costs are added in.

Why a contractor's rate should be higher

A common source of friction is that contractors charge more per hour than the equivalent salaried employee's hourly rate — and that's correct, not greed. A contractor receives no paid holiday, no sick pay, no benefits, no pension, no job security and no employer-paid taxes; they cover all of it themselves and absorb the gaps between contracts. To end up in the same place as a salaried worker, they must charge a premium that funds everything the employer would otherwise provide. When comparing an employee's hourly-equivalent to a contractor's rate, the fair comparison isn't rate to rate — it's the contractor's rate against the employer's fully-loaded cost of the employee.

Overtime, part-time and irregular hours

The clean salary-to-hourly conversion assumes a standard full-time schedule, and real work often isn't that. Overtime can be paid at a premium, changing the effective hourly value of extra hours. Part-time and irregular schedules break the 2,080-hour assumption entirely, so the naive divide misrepresents them. And salaried roles that quietly demand sixty-hour weeks have a real hourly rate far below their apparent one — a fact worth calculating before you accept a role that looks generous on an annual basis but grim once you divide by the hours it actually consumes. Always convert using the hours the job really takes, not the hours it claims to.

Don't forget the unpaid extras around the job

Two jobs at the same salary can pay very differently once the edges are counted. A long unpaid commute is time the job consumes without compensating, effectively lowering your true hourly value; remote or nearby work quietly hands that time back. So do flexible hours, a shorter working week, or a role that respects boundaries versus one that expects evenings and weekends "because you're salaried." None of these show up in a salary-to-hourly divide, yet they can matter more than a modest pay difference. When you compare offers, convert both to a real hourly value that accounts for the total time each genuinely demands — commute, overtime, on-call — not just the contracted hours, and the "lower" salary sometimes turns out to pay better per hour of your life.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert salary to hourly?

Divide the annual salary by your total working hours (hours per week × weeks worked per year). This tool also shows the weekly and monthly equivalent.

How many work hours are in a year?

A common full-time figure is 2,080 (40 hours × 52 weeks), but subtract holidays and time off for a realistic number — which changes the hourly rate.

Why does a contractor charge more per hour?

Salaried roles include paid leave, benefits and employer taxes. A contractor covers all that themselves, so a fair contract rate is higher than the salary-equivalent hourly.
