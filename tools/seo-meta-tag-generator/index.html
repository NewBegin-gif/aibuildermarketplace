Why meta tags still decide who clicks

Your meta title and description are the closest thing you have to a free advertisement on the search results page. Google may rewrite them, and the description isn't a ranking factor — but together they still do a job nothing else does: they convince a human to choose your result over the nine others on the page. Two pages can rank in the same position and earn wildly different traffic purely because one wrote a snippet worth clicking and the other let Google guess. Closing that gap is one of the cheapest wins in all of SEO, because it improves the pages you already have without writing a single new word of content.

The title tag: your headline in the results

The title tag is the bold, clickable line searchers actually read, and it carries real SEO weight — it's one of the clearest signals Google has about what a page is about. So your primary keyword belongs near the front, phrased the way a real person would search for it. But the title is also a headline competing for attention, so it has to promise something. The strongest titles pair the keyword with a reason to click: a concrete benefit, a number, the current year, or a qualifier like "honest," "free," or "for small teams." Lead with the term, follow with the hook, and add your brand at the end only if there's room. If you have to choose between fitting the brand and fitting the benefit, the benefit wins every time.

The description: 155 characters of pure persuasion

The meta description doesn't move rankings, but it's prime persuasion real estate — think of it as the subheadline under your title. It should expand on the promise, answer the searcher's unspoken question ("will this actually help me?"), and end with a gentle nudge to click. Write it for one specific person with one specific problem, not for a crowd. Vague, keyword-stuffed descriptions get ignored — or, worse, replaced by Google with a random sentence pulled from your page, which is almost never the sentence you would have chosen to sell the click.

Length, truncation and the pixel trap

The character counts everyone quotes — roughly 60 for titles, 155 to 160 for descriptions — are rules of thumb, not hard limits. Google actually truncates by pixel width, so a title full of wide characters (W, M, capital letters) gets cut off sooner than one made of narrow ones. That's why this tool shows the count live as you type: the goal isn't to hit an exact number, it's to keep the important words in front of the cut-off point. Put the payload — your keyword and your benefit — early, so that even a snippet Google trims still makes complete sense to someone scanning the page.

Match intent, don't just insert keywords

The single biggest snippet mistake is writing for the algorithm instead of the human. Repeating the same keyword three times looks like spam and reads worse. What genuinely lifts click-through is matching search intent. If someone searches "best CRM for small business," they want a comparison, so your snippet should signal a comparison — not read like a product landing page. Before you write, look at what already ranks for your term: the format Google rewards is usually visible in the top handful of results. Then write a snippet that fits that intent while standing out from the near-identical competition around it. Being the one honest, specific result in a sea of generic ones is often all the edge you need.

The mistakes that quietly cost you clicks

A few errors recur constantly. Duplicate titles across many pages, so Google can't tell them apart and picks one to bury. Titles that are all brand and no benefit, like "Home | Acme Inc." Descriptions left blank, handing Google the pen to write your ad for you. ALL-CAPS or emoji-stuffed snippets that trip spam filters and erode trust. And titles that over-promise, winning the click but losing the visitor the instant the page fails to deliver — which teaches Google, through your bounce rate, that the click wasn't deserved. Every one of these is invisible until you audit for it, which is exactly why writing the snippet deliberately before you publish beats scrambling to fix it after the traffic disappoints.

Titles and descriptions are a system, not a one-off

Getting one page right is a craft you can learn in an afternoon. Getting a whole site right is an operations problem. Every new blog post, product page and category page needs its own unique, intent-matched snippet, and hand-writing each one doesn't scale past a few dozen pages. That's where templates come in: a well-designed pattern such as "{Post Title} — {Benefit} | {Brand}" auto-fills a decent snippet for every page, which you then hand-polish only where the traffic justifies it. The 80/20 rule applies hard here — a handful of pages earn most of your search traffic, so those deserve bespoke snippets, while the long tail can safely run on smart templates.

Let the data tell you which snippets to rewrite

The best part of snippet work is that it's measurable and reversible. Google Search Console shows the impressions and click-through rate for every query your pages appear on, which turns snippet-writing from guesswork into a feedback loop. Sort your pages by impressions, then look for the ones ranking decently but earning a low click-through rate — those are pages people see and skip, almost always because the snippet isn't selling the click. Rewrite the title and description, wait a couple of weeks, and watch whether the rate improves. It's one of the few SEO changes where you can draw a fairly direct line from a specific edit to a specific result, so prioritise the high-impression, low-CTR pages and you'll move real traffic without touching your rankings at all.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a meta title be?

Aim for under about 60 characters (Google truncates longer titles). Put your most important keyword near the front and keep it readable, not stuffed.

Does the meta description affect rankings?

Not directly, but it strongly affects click-through rate — and a higher CTR can indirectly help. Write it like ad copy: clear value, ~150–160 characters, unique per page.

Why does Google sometimes rewrite my snippet?

Google may replace your title or description if it thinks another part of the page better matches the query. A well-written, relevant tag is more likely to be kept.
