What a slug is and why it matters

A slug is the human-readable part of a URL that identifies a specific page — in "example.com/blog/best-crm-for-startups," the slug is "best-crm-for-startups." It seems like a tiny detail, but it's one of the few on-page elements that's visible to both search engines and people before they even click. A clean, descriptive slug tells a searcher exactly what they'll get and gives search engines another clear signal about the page's topic. A messy one — full of numbers, symbols or random characters — does the opposite, looking untrustworthy in the results and telling crawlers nothing.

This tool turns any title into a tidy slug instantly, so that small, permanent detail is right from the start.

URLs are read by humans, not just machines

People genuinely read URLs. They appear in search results under the title, in shared links, in browser address bars, and in the little previews that pop up on hover. A URL like "/best-crm-for-startups" reassures a reader that the link goes where they expect; a URL like "/p?id=48213&cat=7" tells them nothing and feels faintly risky. Clean slugs also survive being copied and pasted into an email or a chat, where the URL is often all the context the recipient gets.

Treat the slug as part of your page's first impression, because for anyone scanning a results page or a shared link, that's exactly what it is.

The anatomy of a good slug

A strong slug follows a few consistent rules. Keep it short — a handful of meaningful words beats a whole sentence. Include the primary keyword, because it's a genuine, if modest, ranking signal and it matches what people search. Use hyphens between words, never underscores or spaces, since search engines treat hyphens as word separators and underscores as joiners. Keep everything lowercase to avoid the duplicate-URL headaches that mixed case can cause on some servers.

And drop the filler: little words like "a," "the," "and" and "of" usually add length without adding meaning, so a slug reads cleaner and sharper without them.

Accents, symbols and other languages

Real titles are messy — they contain apostrophes, ampersands, accented letters, em dashes and emoji, none of which belong in a slug. The safe approach is to transliterate accented characters to their plain equivalents (é becomes e, ü becomes u), strip punctuation and symbols entirely, and collapse the spaces into hyphens. That keeps the URL clean, universally typeable and free of the encoded gibberish (like %E2%80%99) that raw special characters turn into.

For non-Latin scripts you have a real choice — a transliterated Latin slug is broadly compatible and shareable, while a native-script slug can read better to that specific audience — but within a single language, plain and hyphenated is almost always the right call.

The one rule that trips everyone up: don't change a live slug

Here's the mistake that quietly costs traffic: changing the slug of a page that's already published and indexed. The old URL is what search engines have indexed, what other sites have linked to, and what people have bookmarked. Change it without a plan and every one of those links now points at a dead 404, and the ranking equity those links carried evaporates. If you absolutely must change a slug, you have to set up a 301 redirect from the old URL to the new one, so both search engines and visitors are forwarded seamlessly.

The far easier path is to get the slug right before you publish — which is exactly what generating it up front lets you do.

Slugs, folders and overall URL structure

A slug lives inside a larger URL structure, and the two should work together. Shallow, logical folder paths — "/blog/", "/guides/", "/products/" — help both users and crawlers understand where a page sits, while deeply nested paths five levels down add length and fragility for little benefit. A common debate is whether to keep dates in blog URLs; unless the content is genuinely news, leaving them out keeps evergreen posts from looking stale and lets you update them freely.

The guiding principle is that someone should be able to read your URL and roughly understand the page's place in your site without ever loading it.

Why permanence is the whole point

The reason slugs deserve care out of all proportion to their size is that they're meant to be permanent. A title can be tweaked, a meta description rewritten, a paragraph updated — all with zero downside. A slug, once it's live and earning links, is effectively load-bearing: everything pointing at it depends on it not moving. That permanence is a feature, not a burden, but it means the few seconds you spend getting the slug right at creation pay off for the entire life of the page. Cheap to do well once; expensive to fix later.

Match the slug to the title, not the whim

A good habit is to derive the slug from the page's title, then trim it to its essential keywords rather than inventing something unrelated. If your post is titled "The 7 Best CRMs for Startups in 2026," a slug of "best-crms-for-startups" captures the meaning while dropping the number and year that might date it. Keeping the slug and the visible title conceptually aligned means a reader who sees one can predict the other, which builds the small, cumulative trust that keeps people clicking.

Avoid the opposite temptation too: cramming extra keywords into a slug that aren't in the content reads as manipulative and helps nothing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a URL slug?

The readable part of a URL after the domain, like 'best-crm-tools'. A clean slug helps both users and search engines understand the page.

Should I use hyphens or underscores?

Hyphens. Google treats hyphens as word separators but underscores as joiners, so 'crm-tools' is read as two words while 'crm_tools' isn't.

Can I change a slug later?

You can, but set up a 301 redirect from the old URL or you'll lose its rankings and break links. An SEO plugin like Rank Math manages redirects.
